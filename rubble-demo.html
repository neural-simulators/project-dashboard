Rubble demo

A rescue robot clearing rubble without a second collapse: which pull is safe, which piece is heavy or pinned, brace or let go.

Ground truth is the physics engine (PhysX in NVIDIA Isaac Sim), run with the true hidden masses and pins. The robot only sees the pile's shapes and positions.

Updated 19 Oct 2026, 14:22.

Can the learned simulator answer the rescuer's questions?↑ contents

Question. A rescue robot has to clear a rubble pile over a trapped person without bringing it down. Can a learned simulator, trained only on simple parts of such piles, tell the robot which pull brings the pile down, which piece is secretly heavy or pinned, and whether to brace or let go when the pile starts to move, at least 90% as often as the physics engine says, and with honest uncertainty?

Answer so far.

  • At the bar on the three decisions, after two rounds of training on simple parts only (665 single structures and small groups; no test pile seen). The best learned simulator (regression) agrees with the physics engine on "will this pull bring the pile down?" 90% of the time once it has done its test pulls on that piece (85% before any test pull), on "will this pull free the piece?" 92%, and on "is this stuck piece heavy or pinned?" 92%. The first is right at the bar (95% interval 89-91%), so round 3 adds more training parts.
  • Honest uncertainty: the chances it states are much closer to what happens than always quoting the base rate (Brier score 0.09 against 0.18).
  • Fewer attempts, no collapse: trying to free one piece, it brings the pile down in 0 of 204 piles and needs 1.02 pulls; without the test pulls 4% and 1.18 pulls; choosing at random 43% and 1.9 pulls.
  • Weak spot: pinned pieces. When nothing moves even under the strongest test pull, it says pinned only 62% of the time (heavy pieces: 98%); most stuck pieces are heavy, so this barely shows in the 92%.
  • Mid-slide, its choice between letting go and bracing stops the pile 92% of the time (always letting go: 78%); it braces in a third of the cases, and a fifth need it.
  • Flow matching, which gives more realistic motion, is 5 points behind on the pile question (85% after test pulls) and passes the other two (91%, 93%).
  • What helped most is more training parts (chart below); targeted parts (crowded debris, pairs of structures, the robot's own pull strengths) were added after looking at the errors.

What we changed, round by round↑ contents

Accuracy on "will this pull bring the pile down?" (before acting) after each round, against the number of training parts; each round on the held-out piles answered at the time (48 piles for round 0, 150-203 after).

robot's view told the hidden masses
  • Round 0: the V5 models as they are (never trained on rubble). All fail: "will this pull bring the pile down?" 26-42% (always "stays up": 74%); freeing one piece they bring the pile down 40-54% of the time, no better than random (48 piles). The plain step-wise models (without the physics-guided extras) do no better (flow 26%, regression 58%).
  • Round 1: fine-tuned on 113 single-structure parts (28,600 pulls in the physics engine), 8,000 steps: 72-77% before acting.
  • Found in round 1: false alarms were mostly debris the model moved 20 cm where the engine moved it 0, after a pier brick was pulled (the brick slid out in the model but was held by the weight above) or a neighbouring piece was pulled; misses were mostly loaded bricks the model held still.
  • Round 2a: 381 parts (more single structures, plus parts with crowded debris and pairs of structures side by side): 81-84% before acting.
  • Round 2b: 665 parts, adding parts where half the pulls are the robot's own standard and test pulls: 82-85% before acting, 85-90% after test pulls.
  • How the robot reasons (the same for every model, set before scoring): test pulls go gentle, firm, strong until the piece moves 5 cm; distances are compared on a log scale (a factor of 2 counts as one standard deviation); the pile question can be answered after the piece's own test pulls.
  • Fixed on the way: the robot's view leaked the true mass of hidden heavy pieces to the learned simulator (an engine record in the scene file), and guesses about masses did not reach it; found because predictions for 3-6 times heavier were identical. A strong test pull (8 times) was added because half of the heavy pieces did not move under the firm one, so heavy and pinned could not be told apart.
  • Round 3 (running): more parts of all three kinds, and a control that only trains longer on the round-2b parts.

How it is measured

Test piles
two structures side by side (a slab on brick piers, a slab propped on a pier, or a slab on two beams across piers) over a red figure, with loose debris on top. Some pieces are secretly 3-6 times heavier than they look; in about a third of the piles one slab or beam is pinned (fixed). None of these piles is used for training.
Training parts
one structure, 0-2 debris pieces, the figure half the time; about 250 pulls each in the physics engine. The learned simulator never sees a full test pile.
What the robot is told
every piece's shape, size and position, and a mass guessed from its look (size times the density of concrete, brick or wood). Not told: which pieces are heavier, which one is pinned.
Standard pull
a piece pulled out along its length for 1 s with twice the force its look suggests (its weight times its friction).
Comes down (collapse)
after the pull, another piece moved more than 5 cm or tilted more than 20 degrees, or the figure moved more than 1 cm.
Chance it comes down
the robot samples 12 guesses about the hidden masses and pins and asks the learned simulator 4 times for each: the share of predictions in which the pile comes down.
Heavy or pinned
for pieces that are secretly heavy or pinned: does the robot, after its test pulls, pick the right one of the two (the grey mark is always saying heavy)? "Normal, heavy or pinned" asks the three-way question for every piece (grey mark: always saying normal).
Test pulls
short pulls of 0.3 s, gentle (1.2 times the force its look suggests), then firm (4 times), then strong (8 times), stopping as soon as the piece moves 5 cm or the robot is 95% sure. The robot compares how far the piece moved with the learned simulator's prediction for each guess (normal, 3, 4, 5 or 6 times heavier, pinned); the guesses that match best win.
Comes down, after test pulls
the same question answered after the piece's own test pulls: each guess about the piece counts as much as it explains what the test pulls showed.
After one pull (what-if)
the robot sees what one pull did, keeps the guesses that explain it, and answers for another piece from the same start.
Brace or let go
from the moment another piece starts to move during a pull that brings the pile down: either stop pulling (let go), or stop pulling and hold the fastest-moving piece where it is (brace). Stopped = nothing else moves more than 5 cm afterwards. The robot lets go unless it predicts the pile keeps moving, then braces; its choice is right if that action stops the pile (the grey mark is always letting go).
Free one piece
the robot pulls pieces one at a time, in order of how sure it is that the pull frees the piece without a collapse; a piece that does not move is set aside and the next one tried; a collapse ends the attempt. Compared with the same order without the test pulls and with random order.
Accuracy
share of questions where the robot's answer (chance above or below 50%) matches the physics engine. The grey mark is the accuracy of always giving the most common answer.
Calibration
Brier score: mean squared difference between the stated chance and what happened (0 is perfect; always saying the base rate scores base rate times one minus base rate).
