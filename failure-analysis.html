Failure analysis

Why the learned simulators fail, and whether more data or extra rules is the fix.

Ground truth is the physics engine (PhysX in NVIDIA Isaac Sim). Every number below compares a learned simulator's prediction with the engine's motion from the same start and action.

Updated 18 Oct 2026, 17:24.

Is it a data problem, or do the models need extra rules?↑ contents

Question. If a model gets plenty of examples of one kind of scene, does it learn that interaction on its own, or does it need the extra rules (losses) we have been adding? In other words: are the extra rules only making up for too little data?

Answer.

  • Yes, for what happens at the moment of contact. With enough examples of one interaction, a plain model learns it with no extra rules: the cone stops at the wall instead of passing through, the struck domino falls correctly. The model with all the extra rules is no better there.
  • No, for the main failure. The models predict 0.2 s at a time and feed each prediction back in to get 3 s. Small errors in position and speed feed each other and grow, even on the very examples the model was trained on. More examples of the same kind do not fix this, and neither do the extra rules.
  • Training on the model's own mistakes (letting the physics engine continue from the slightly wrong states the model produces) did not fix it: in all four scenes it was worse than simply training longer on the original data. The remaining suspect is how precisely each step predicts speed, which is passed on from step to step. Two tests are running (below).

What we found↑ contents

1. Each 0.2 s step is right; the error grows when predictions are fed back

Started from the real state, each step is a few centimetres off (about 2 cm in free motion, 6 cm at a collision). Fed back step after step, the error reaches about 1.5 m by 3 s. Putting the real speed back at every step keeps it at 18 cm, so the growth comes from wrong position and speed feeding each other.

Own predictions fed back (the normal 3 s prediction) Fed back, but the real speed put back at every step Every step started from the real state

2. More examples of one scene teach the moment of contact, without extra rules

Trained on about 1,900 pushes of the cone from different spots, a plain model stops the cone at the wall instead of pushing it through, which the main models do. The struck domino is learned to 3 cm. With all the extra rules the cone is not better (21 cm error against 18 cm without them).

Held-out push of the cone into the wall panel: top left the physics engine, then the five models trained on this scene. All stop the cone at the wall.

3. More examples do not stop the fed-back error from growing

On the tennis ball scene, each step is about 1 cm off, yet the 3 s prediction ends 34 cm off on the training examples themselves (85 cm by the end of a long roll), and every model sends the beach ball the wrong way. A model that predicts all 3 s at once, with nothing fed back, cannot fit the whole trajectory either (69 cm).

Held-out push of the tennis ball into the beach ball: the physics engine (top left) moves the beach ball down-left; every model sends it somewhere else.

4. On the real test set, the drift starts at collisions, at speed and when rolling

Across 15,000 test simulations, the error is 0.28 m when the pushed object touches nothing and 0.61 m with five or more contacts, 0.70 m above 3 m/s, 0.62 m for balls. A quarter of the objects that should be knocked away are barely moved, like the bucket the basketball should knock over.

Basketball dropped on the bucket (test scene): the physics engine knocks the bucket over and the ball rolls away; regression, flow matching and diffusion leave the bucket standing.

5. Speed, not position, is what is imprecise

Even when a step starts from the real state, the predicted speed is off by 0.1 m/s (tennis ball) to 0.3 m/s (cone), and by about 0.5 m/s at collisions. Over one 0.2 s step that is only 1-2 cm of position, so each step looks right; but the speed is passed on to the next step and adds up over 3 s. The training loss counts speed errors about 4x less than position errors.

What we are testing now↑ contents

Tried: training on the model's own mistakes (three rounds, each against a control trained just as long on the original data). It made things slightly worse in every scene (tennis ball 39 cm against 31 cm for the control), while plain extra training kept improving. Now running: (1) the same models with speed errors weighted 10x more in the loss; (2) the plain model trained 8x longer on one scene, to see whether it can memorise its own training examples at all. How it is measured

From the true start
each 0.2 s block predicted from the physics engine's state at its start (no chaining): position error of objects moving in the block, at its end.
Chained
the normal rollout: each block starts from the model's own prediction of the previous one.
Chained, true velocity / true position
chained, but at every block start the velocities and spins (or the positions and orientations) are replaced by the physics engine's.
First goes wrong
the first moment any object is more than 10 cm from where the physics engine has it, and what happened to that object in the 0.2 s before: a contact started (with a fixed or a moving object), nothing new (free motion, including sliding and rolling), or the action itself.
Response
the acted object's change of velocity across its first contact in the prediction, as a share of the true change (1 = right; 0.5 = half as strong).
Knock-ons missed
objects the physics engine moves more than 5 cm that the prediction moves less than half as far.
Single-scene overfitting
one scene, about 2,400 simulations of one interaction (many start positions, directions, speeds); 80% train the model, 20% are held out. Copies of test scenes in a separate split, never used for the main models.
DAgger
the model's own rollouts on training actions give drifted states; the physics engine continues 0.2 s from each, and those blocks are added to training (Ross et al. 2011; Venkatraman et al. 2015). The control is fine-tuned for the same steps on the original data only.
