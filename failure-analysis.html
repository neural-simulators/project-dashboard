Failure analysis

Why the learned simulators fail: measured clip by clip, on the whole test set, one step at a time, and on single scenes they should be able to learn.

Ground truth is the physics engine (PhysX in NVIDIA Isaac Sim). Every number below compares a learned simulator's prediction with the engine's motion from the same start and action.

Summary↑ contents

  • Each 0.2 s step is learned well: started from the physics engine's true state, V5 regression is 2.4 cm off in free motion and 6.4 cm at a contact with a fixed object (2,000 test simulations).
  • Rollouts fail because the error compounds from block to block: chained, the same model is 152 cm off by the last block. Restoring the true velocity at every block start cuts this to 18 cm, restoring the true position to 11 cm, so the error grows through the loop between the predicted position and velocity.
  • Contacts, speed and rolling start the drift: position error grows from 0.28 m when the acted object touches nothing to 0.61 m with 5 or more contacts, 0.70 m above 3 m/s, 0.62 m for balls. In the clips the prediction first goes wrong at the acted object's first contact or right after a spin, and collision responses are 30-90% of the true one.
  • The physics losses and collision guidance act within one block started from the true state, where the models are already accurate; none acts on the loop between blocks, which is why they did not remove these failures.
  • Even on a single scene with about 1,900 training simulations of one interaction, the step-wise models fit each step (0.5-4 cm from the true start) but not their own training rollouts (tennis ball 34 cm, cone 17 cm); a one-shot model, which has no chaining, cannot fit the whole 3 s at once either (19-69 cm). Short domino strikes fit (3 cm).
  • Being tested: DAgger, training on the model's own drifted states with the physics engine's continuation as the target (results below as the rounds finish).

  • 82% of V5 regression rollouts go more than 10 cm wrong, at a median of 0.33 s; 46% first go wrong in free motion (sliding or rolling with slightly wrong deceleration), 27% at a contact, 9% right after the action.
  • The more contacts and the faster the action, the worse: 0.28 m with no contact, 0.32 m with one, 0.48 m with 2-4, 0.61 m with 5 or more (98% go wrong); 0.22 m below 1 m/s, 0.70 m above 3 m/s.
  • 26% of knock-ons are missed (struck objects not moved enough) and 40% of collision responses are off by more than half; balls (0.62 m) and throws (0.64 m) are the hardest, drops (0.21 m) and placements (0.16 m) the easiest.
  • Flow matching, diffusion and the new V3 show the same pattern with larger errors.

Clip by clip: Improved Results V4 (overfitting scenes)↑ contents

  • Brick slid toward the wall without touching it (3 clips): fitted, 0-2 cm.
  • Golf ball dropped on the crate (4): goes wrong at the first touch of the crate (0.27-0.40 s); the bounce is wrong, the ball travels 0.55-0.9 m instead of 0.2-0.5 m and ends inside the crate.
  • Skittle knocked over (4): goes wrong at the first collision (0.1-0.4 s); the response is 60-80% of the true one and the skittle ends at 6-19 degrees instead of 104.
  • Domino chain (4): goes wrong while the first domino tips (0.2-0.5 s), before the next contact; the chain falls short.
  • Barrel spun into the wall and the four fast spins: go wrong right after the action (0.03-0.1 s): rolling and tumbling on the floor are wrong from the first block.
  • Bowling pin thrown at the bucket: goes wrong at the pin's first bounce on the floor (0.13 s), velocity change 1.2-1.8x too large, then it overlaps the bucket by 17-22 cm. Tray hitting the die: the die is sent off at the wrong speed and direction (0.07 s).
  • The training-action clips fail the same way as the held-out ones, and every overfitting scene contains several contacts, which is what fails.

Three rounds per scene for plain regression and the full V5 model: the model's rollouts on the training actions give drifted states at random blocks (2 per rollout), the physics engine continues 0.2 s from each (states with objects pushed into each other are rejected by the energy check, 7-8%), those blocks are added to the training data (aggregated over rounds) and the model is fine-tuned for 4,000 steps; the control is fine-tuned for the same steps on the original data only. How it is measured↑ contents

From the true start
each 0.2 s block predicted from the physics engine's state at its start (no chaining): position error of objects moving in the block, at its end.
Chained
the normal rollout: each block starts from the model's own prediction of the previous one.
Chained, true velocity / true position
chained, but at every block start the velocities and spins (or the positions and orientations) are replaced by the physics engine's.
First goes wrong
the first moment any object is more than 10 cm from where the physics engine has it, and what happened to that object in the 0.2 s before: a contact started (with a fixed or a moving object), nothing new (free motion, including sliding and rolling), or the action itself.
Response
the acted object's change of velocity across its first contact in the prediction, as a share of the true change (1 = right; 0.5 = half as strong).
Knock-ons missed
objects the physics engine moves more than 5 cm that the prediction moves less than half as far.
Single-scene overfitting
one scene, about 2,400 simulations of one interaction (many start positions, directions, speeds); 80% train the model, 20% are held out. Copies of test scenes in a separate split, never used for the main models.
DAgger
the model's own rollouts on training actions give drifted states; the physics engine continues 0.2 s from each, and those blocks are added to training (Ross et al. 2011; Venkatraman et al. 2015). The control is fine-tuned for the same steps on the original data only.
