End-to-end study

From LLM-written physics tasks to choosing actions with a learned simulator, with PhysX as the ground truth at every step.

Why run the loop end to end?

The project's goal is a planner that asks a fast learned simulator "what happens if I do this?" instead of running the physics engine. This study builds that whole loop at small scale and checks every link against PhysX: language models design scenes and write tasks, a language model judges outcomes from images, language models propose actions, and learned future-state models preview those actions before PhysX decides. It shows which links already hold and which break.

Two LLMs write physics tasks for scenes they design in Isaac Sim. PhysX checks every task and is the ground truth for everything below: which tasks are valid, how hard they are, whether a reviewer's verdict is right, and whether a chosen action succeeds.

Status: wave 1 complete: 165 valid test tasks; four LLM proposers, four trained future-state models, counterfactual sweeps, closed-loop planning; updated 13 Oct 2026, 19:06. Sections fill in as runs finish.

Key results

  • GPT-5.4 is the strongest zero-shot action proposer: its first choice succeeds on 56% of tasks, against 35% for Qwen3-VL-30B Thinking and 12% for Qwen3-VL-30B Instruct and Qwen2.5-7B, which do worse than one random action (19%).
  • GPT-5.4 is also the best scene writer (10 of 10 scenes built, against 0-2 of 10 for Qwen3-VL-30B) and a reliable reviewer when it sees object states and the simulator's definitions (89% agreement with PhysX on its own tasks).
  • The trained future-state models learn the moved object's motion but not what happens to the objects it hits. As action choosers they lift 64 random candidates from 19% to 29-35% and GPT-5.4's candidates from 56% to 59-61%, far below what PhysX itself reaches (93% and 68%).
  • In a closed loop (preview, revise, submit), PhysX previews let GPT-5.4 solve 87% of tasks and a trained model's previews 61-73%, with 3-4x the tokens and 2.5-3x the LLM time per success.

  • With PhysX previews GPT-5.4 solves 87% of tasks (its zero-shot first choice: 56%), 49% on the first preview. With a trained model's previews it solves 61-73% (Transformer best).
  • On the tasks solved both ways, a model preview makes each success cost more: 4 previews instead of 1, 3-4x the tokens (17-23k vs 6k) and 2.5-3x the LLM time (13-16 s vs 5-6 s). The model is quicker than PhysX per task (Transformer 0.07 s, flow matching and diffusion 1 s, PhysX 1-2 s with scene load), but LLM time dominates.
  • Model previews mislead: GPT-5.4 stops early with an action that fails in PhysX on 23-38% of runs (9% with PhysX previews), and on 13-16% it had previewed a real solution that the model showed as failing. Composite tasks: 64% with PhysX previews, 7-43% with a model's.

  • MOVi (Bullet), started from the same state with the same convex shapes: Isaac agrees for the first half second (1-11 cm), then the paths split after collisions between these large (0.7-2 m) tossed objects: 0.3-1.5 m apart at 2 s, and 1.3-1.9 m with our training settings (angular damping 2.0).
  • Physion (Unity PhysX): the domino chain agrees throughout (2 cm) and a dropped bowl for its first half second, but fast pushes differ almost at once (0.3-0.7 m at 0.5 s): Physion's 9.7 m/s ball loses about 1 m/s in its first 0.1 s, and the room's walls, floor material and drag are not in the files. Training on these data needs those settings matched, or only the first moments of each trial.
  • Scene types worth adding to our suite: towers near tipping (Support), multi-step domino chains, bowls and cups (Contain), and objects with hidden mass, friction or bounce that the planner must infer from a first push (Physion++).

Setup

  • Actions (one per task, applied at t = 0): place, drop, throw (velocity, from where the object rests unless stated), push (velocity), spin (angular velocity); place, drop and throw may set a heading. PhysX runs 3 s at 120 Hz.
  • Task writers: GPT-5.4 (Azure, default reasoning) and Qwen3-VL-30B-A3B; each designs scenes, then one task per category per scene. Success conditions are trees of checkable predicates (distance, contact, inside, on top of, toppled, at rest, ...).
  • Future-state models (MLP and Transformer trained by regression; the same Transformer trained by flow matching and by classic diffusion): same inputs (object tokens: shape, size, mass, friction, bounciness, start pose, action) and output (each movable object's position, orientation and contacts every 0.1 s for 11 s). Trained on rollouts from scenes that are not in the task suite.
  • Numbers on this page are refreshed as runs finish; sections without enough data say so.
  • Each task's action space follows its instruction: throw, push and spin start where the object rests unless the instruction says otherwise; place and drop release points are chosen anywhere or in a stated region; the heading is chosen only in orient tasks. Planners see only the scene and the instruction.
  • Difficulty: share of 64 random actions from the task's action space that succeed. Easy: at least 13 of 64; hard: at most 5 of 64, including tasks solved only by 160 refinement tries; medium in between. Tasks with no solution found are listed separately.
