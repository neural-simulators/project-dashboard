Video models

Open video diffusion models continuing the physics test tasks from one frame and a sentence, against the physics engine.

Can an off-the-shelf video diffusion model continue a physics scene from one frame and a sentence?

The learned simulators on the other tabs predict object states. Here the same frozen test tasks are given to four open video diffusion models (the open models of the Principia benchmark, Thozhiyoor et al. 2026): each gets the first rendered frame of the PhysX rollout and a text prompt, and must produce the next 5 seconds of video. The physics engine's own render of the rollout is the ground truth, and the models are scored in image space: the task's objects are tracked in every video with SAM 2 and compared with the exact projected PhysX motion.

Two prompts per task: the action prompt says only what is done at t = 0 (the information a learned simulator receives), the outcome prompt adds what actually happens in the rollout. Where the co-author's V5 learned simulator was rendered on the same clips, it is shown and scored alongside, by the same tracker.

Takeaways↑ contents

  • Too slow and too timid. On the pilot clips both Wan models move the acted object at a fraction of the physics engine's pace (median time scale 0.0 to 0.3, where 1 is right) and leave it frozen in a third to a half of the videos; our V5 regression predictor, rendered on the same clips, keeps half the pace (median 0.5) and the right direction (cosine 0.82 against 0.14 to 0.32 for the video models).
  • Telling the model what happens makes it move more, not move right. The outcome prompt roughly doubles the amount and pace of motion for both models but leaves the direction near chance and the frozen share unchanged, and it triples the share of deformed or duplicated objects for the 5B model (12% to 36%): the extra text is acted out by morphing objects rather than by physics.
  • Mechanisms break the models. Pendulums and springs are the clearest failures: the 5B model turned a pendulum bob into a cabinet that landed on the dominoes, the 14B model turned the string into a rod and lost the bob; dominoes deform into a curved strip or are knocked over with nothing touching them.
  • Drops and slides come closest. Free-falling and sliding objects are moved in roughly the right direction, but 2 to 3 times too slowly, so the collision that defines the task arrives late or not at all within the 3 s of the rollout.

How each model behaves↑ contents

Wan2.2 TI2V-5B

  • Pilot (33 clips): median time scale 0.02 (action prompt) and 0.09 (outcome), 45% frozen, direction cosine 0.14 / 0.22. Prone to inventing objects: a second bucket, a cabinet in place of a pendulum bob; deformation rises sharply with the outcome prompt (12% to 36%).
  • Fastest of the four models (about 2 min per 5 s clip on an H100, 3.5 min on an RTX PRO 6000) at 1088 x 800.

Wan2.2 I2V-A14B

  • Pilot (24 / 15 clips): median time scale 0.00 / 0.27, 33 to 40% frozen, direction cosine 0.23 / 0.32; keeps object identity somewhat better than the 5B model but still loses a task object in a fifth to a quarter of the videos (left the frame or vanished).
  • Knock-on motion without a cause (dominoes falling before anything reaches them) and strings turning into rigid rods are its typical errors.

Failure patterns across models

  • Frozen: the acted object never sets off although the prompt names the action; the most common failure for both Wan models, in a third to a half of the pilot clips.
  • Slow motion: when objects do move, they move at a fraction of the true pace, so the contact that the task is about arrives late or never.
  • Identity: objects change shape, split, duplicate or dissolve, most often the small thin ones (dominoes, pendulum bobs, strings) and most often with the longer outcome prompt.

How to read the tables↑ contents

Actor error
average distance between the tracked centre of the acted object in the video and the exact projected PhysX centre, over the 3 s of the rollout, as a share of the frame diagonal (480 x 360 px, D = 600 px; lower is better). The same tracker on the physics engine's own render gives the floor.
Final error
the same distance at 3 s.
Time scale
how fast the acted object moves along its PhysX path: every video frame is matched to the PhysX time whose position is nearest (monotonically), and the slope of matched PhysX time against video time is fitted while PhysX still moves. 1 = the right pace; 0.3 = three times too slow; 0 = it never sets off.
Motion ratio
length of the acted object's tracked path divided by the length of its PhysX path over 3 s (1 = the right amount of motion).
Direction
average cosine between the tracked and the PhysX displacement over 0.2 s windows in which PhysX moves the object by more than 1% of D (1 = same direction, 0 = unrelated, below 0 = opposite).
Knock-ons
of the other task objects that PhysX moves by more than 3% of D, the share that the video moves by at least half as much.
Frozen
share of videos in which the acted object moves less than 2% of D while PhysX moves it more than 8% of D.
Vanished / left frame / deformed
share of videos in which a task object's mask at 3 s is smaller than 20% of its mask at 0 s while the object was last seen well inside the picture (vanished: it disappeared, merged or the tracker lost it), or near the picture's edge or where PhysX also puts it outside (left the frame); and larger than 250% (deformed, duplicated or ballooned).
Settling
speed of the acted object in the video after 3 s, when every PhysX object is at rest (share of D per second; 0 is right).
SSIM
structural similarity between the video frame and the physics engine's render at 0.5, 1, 2 and 3 s (480 x 360); 1 = identical. Only where the renders exist (PNG frames).
Reviewer
a vision-language model (Qwen3.8-27B, the same reviewer as on the Improved Results V5 tab) sees the task instruction and five frames (0, 0.5, 1, 2, 3 s) and answers whether the task succeeded, the motion is plausible and every object kept its identity; the same questions on the physics engine's render calibrate it.
