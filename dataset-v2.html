Dataset V2

Forty-six indoor mechanics families in scanned rooms, each simulated as a bundle of counterfactuals, interventions and a control, with timed events and measured questions.

What does a world model need in its training data to learn causes, and not only motion?

The first dataset (the Dataset tab) taught the model to continue motion: one action, one rollout, three seconds. It rarely showed the same world twice with one thing changed, and never showed an action arriving in the middle of a rollout. Dataset V2 is built around those two gaps. Every root scene is simulated as up to eight branches from one manifest: the factual run, three counterfactual worlds (a property, a material, a shape), the removal of a causal link, two timed interventions after an observed prefix, and an unchanged replay. Every branch carries contact events with their impulses, frame tags, physics screens and questions whose answers are measured from the saved states. The motion families were widened to cover the mechanisms published physics datasets list, placed in whole scanned rooms, and driven by a visible actuator wherever one is physically sensible.

1. Why a second dataset↑ contents

The audit of the first dataset (25 October) counted 1,367 paired scenes and 490,679 stored rollouts, all 3 s at 30 Hz, in procedural rooms. Only about 2.5 percent of its training action rows have a counterfactual sibling, and none has an intervention after an observed prefix. A model trained on it sees many motions but almost never the evidence that separates "what happens" from "what would have happened if". The published datasets have the same shape: breadth or scale on one axis, no branches from one world.

1b. What changed from the first dataset↑ contents

The first dataset (Dataset tab) is large and mostly random: each of its scenes was simulated a few hundred times with one randomly drawn action at t = 0, and a template task was written for a few of them. The second dataset is small and authored: every root world is built around a mechanism that is meant to happen, and every action program is simulated as a bundle of branches from the same world. The table puts the two side by side; the census below measures, from the first dataset's own saved states, how many of its simulations show motion that the action caused.

2. One root, eight branches↑ contents

A root is one manifest: the room, the objects with mass, friction, restitution, colliders and materials, the joints and springs, the fields (gravity, drag, wind, buoyancy, thrust) and the actuator's force program. The eight branches are fresh PhysX runs from edited copies of that manifest; nothing is spliced, teleported or driven to a wanted outcome. Each branch exists for a reason and comes with the check that proves it did what it claims.

FactualThe action program in the room: a piston pushes, a gripper releases, a trigger pin is pulled. The run the questions are asked about.Trains: forward prediction with an explicit cause in the scene.
Counterfactual propertyMass, friction, restitution or spring stiffness of the main object edited, everything else kept, same action.Trains: the model must carry latent parameters it cannot see and use them, instead of guessing one average physics (the OneWorld consistency argument).
Counterfactual materialAnother material preset: friction, restitution and appearance change, mass kept.Trains: appearance is a cue for contact behaviour, and a cue only; the same look can hide different dynamics.
Counterfactual shapeSphere to cube, box to sphere or cylinder, domino to peg, at equal volume and mass.Trains: geometry decides rolling, tipping and contact normals; the model must read the collider, not the label.
Removal of a causal linkThe chain's link (a domino, a buffer, a support, a target) is removed before the run; joints and forces referencing it are dropped.Trains: "what caused what". The downstream event should disappear; the pair is the ground truth for explanation questions and for the event head's missed-event comparison.
Intervention while the tool actsThe actuator's force or torque changes while it is still in contact (42 families); for the 14 families without an actuator a declared impulse at the same point.Trains: the action token matters at every step, not only at frame zero; a planner can only use a model that reacts to a changed command.
Late interventionAfter release, at a stated time: an impulse on a free body, a fan starting for the air families, a thrust cut for the rocket, a gravity change.Trains: prediction from an observed prefix plus a new cause. The prefix check proves the branch shares the first t seconds exactly; the effect check proves the cause mattered.
No-change controlAn independent replay of the factual manifest.Trains nothing; it proves the engine is deterministic to 1e-5 m, so every difference between branches is caused by the edit.
Why bundles and not more rollouts. One root with eight branches costs about as much as eight unrelated rollouts, but the eight unrelated rollouts never let a loss compare two futures of the same world. The bundle makes the counterfactual, intervention and explanation questions trainable with paired targets, and gives the uncertainty question (World model tab, section 18) its spread: the same prefix can continue differently when a hidden property differs.

3. The 56 families↑ contents

The registry has eight groups. Collisions and motion (12 families) and mechanisms (13) cover the single-stage mechanics of Physion, MOVi and the mechanical half of PhysInOne's phenomenon list: drops that bounce, billiard breaks, bowling, ramps, slipping to rolling, spinning tops, pendulums (simple, double, rigid rod), springs, pulleys, levers, cranks, catapults, carts. Chain reactions (13) are the new emphasis: multi-stage causal chains such as ramp to spring bumper to dominoes, a spring launcher into a tower, a pendulum that fells a tower whose blocks push a ball into a box, a seesaw that launches a second ball, branching domino networks. Flexible solids (3: trampoline net, diving board on hinges with angular springs, flexible bridge) are coarse elastic assemblies that prepare the format for real deformables. Fields and air (5) add buoyant balloons, a falling sheet with drag and a fan, thrust, changed gravity and an orbit. Circular motion (2) and rotation and inertia (2), added on 3 November, test what a mass-blind model gets wrong: in the first a surface, not a field, supplies the centripetal force (a ball rounds a curved wall and leaves along the tangent; a ball orbits and spirals down a funnel), in the second the spread of mass, not its amount, decides the motion (a ball, a solid cylinder and a hollow tube race down one ramp and finish in that order whatever their masses; masses pulled inward make a spinning rod spin faster, and loose masses slide off it). Their pilots are in section 9c.

Why chains. The main failure of the current predictor is errors compounding over chained blocks (K11 on the World model tab). A single collision does not expose that; a chain does, because a small error in stage one moves stage two by a lot. Nineteen families declare their stage list (ball to ramp, ball to bumper, bumper to first domino, ...), and acceptance requires every stage to touch in the declared order. That makes "did the chain happen" a measured label, usable both as a loss and as the answer to "what will happen next".

3c. Shapes beyond spheres and boxes↑ contents

Most round-2 objects are spheres and boxes (books, dominoes and tower blocks are boxes; pins and pegs are cylinders), and the shape counterfactual swapped between those. The stage exporter already simulates convex hulls, capsules and cylinders exactly, so on 30 October thirteen equal-volume shapes were added to the swap vocabulary, each with a motion of its own: a cone (tips on a nudge, rolls in a circle on its side), a lying capsule (rolls across one axis only), a puck (slides, almost never rolls), a wedge (slides on its flat face, tips over its edge), hexagonal and triangular prisms (roll in jerks), a pyramid, an L block (off-centre mass, asymmetric toppling), a dumbbell (rolls about one axis, rocks about the other), a standing ring (rolls and wobbles, can trap a ball), a bottle (tips, rolls when down), a mug and a bowl (hollow vessels of 13 to 16 convex parts that can catch a small ball). The scaled run draws the actor's shape from this pool in a share of the roots, and a root whose actor is a vocabulary shape gets the family's plain shape as its counterfactual. The one-GPU pilot now queued is their first simulation; the co-author's sixty procedural assets (mugs, bottles, traffic cones, bowling pins, skittles) can enter the same slot once their part lists are scaled to the slot's volume.

Why shapes. Whether an object rolls, slides, tips or rocks is a property the model must read from the motion, not from a silhouette. With nearly every object a ball or a block, a predictor can shortcut on the outline (round rolls, square slides) and never learn that the outcome depends on the contact geometry and the mass distribution. A cone that rolls in a circle, a dumbbell that rocks and an L block that topples toward its heavy side break that shortcut and give the property head (hidden mass distribution) and the shape counterfactual branch something to explain.

3d. Heavy furniture: six families with 15 to 32 kg bodies↑ contents

The moving bodies of the 46 light families weigh 0.05 to 7 kg. The furniture of a digital twin (a scanned room with its chairs, tables and cabinets) weighs 10 to 90 kg, and mass scales both the momentum an object carries and the force needed to move it, so a model trained only on light objects would be extrapolating as soon as it meets a twin. Why heavy bodies, and why these causes. The point is the mass axis of the training distribution, not new mechanics: the same contacts, tips and pushes as in the light families, at ten to a hundred times the mass, so that momentum, impact forces and the force a cause must deliver are seen across the whole range a twin will present. The mass counterfactuals are chosen where mass can flip the outcome: in the chair row, the second chair made four times heavier still fell in the pilot and took the third chair with it, so the question of whether a heavier object stops a chain has a measured answer per record, which is what a model needs to tie mass to behaviour instead of to appearance.

Pilot history: the first two runs failed on how the stage writer names the furniture's visual file (fixed), the third simulated all 43 branches and passed 2 of 6 families, the fourth passed 3 more after the fixes listed above, the fifth redefined the chair row. Next: the room's own ScanNet++ furniture as moving bodies (needs per-object masses and colliders from the twins pipeline), and a size knob plus density-consistent masses for the light families (section 9).

3e. Which shapes and assets fit which family↑ contents

37 of the 56 families have a slot: the one object whose shape the recipe allows to change (usually the moving actor). Each slot was checked against the 16 shapes of 3c and the 73 movable procedural assets of the co-author's catalog (balls, cans, bottles, pins, cones, blocks, prisms, dumbbells, rings, mugs, bowls, trays, planks, and so on), each scaled to the slot's volume with the slot's mass and material kept. A candidate is geometrically applicable when the tool reseats on it, the scene stays in its zone, nothing overlaps at the start and every branch of the bundle still builds. The dynamic tier then simulated one factual run per accepted pair (2,056 runs, then 630 re-runs of twelve families after the sweep itself exposed three seating defects in the swap code: a gripper re-seated after a swap of a different object, hanging bodies swapped by their bottom instead of their centre, an axis-aligned gripper on a slope-aligned payload) and labelled whether the family's motion still happens. The production run samples the slot from the resulting per-family pool: weight 1 when the motion survives, a low weight when it does not, so that draws which break a chain remain in the data as labelled negatives, and negatives together take at most 20 percent of a family's draws.

Why a per-family pool instead of one global vocabulary. A shape that cannot be held on a ramp or that always stalls a chain would otherwise fill the data with scenes where nothing happens, and the model would learn that the family is boring rather than that the shape matters. Measuring applicability per slot keeps every family informative while still letting the shape vary.

4. The actuator↑ contents

Thirty-nine of the 46 light families are set in motion by a visible tool: a piston that pushes, or a gripper that holds an object and releases it, with a force program (start, stop, release time, force or torque). What the engine simulates is the tool alone, a single rigid box with a known force schedule; the yellow arm in the renders is drawn, not simulated, and the tool is held kinematically after release. The seven remaining families (top, two tires, pulley loads, rigid-rod pendulum, double pendulum, projectile, scanned chair) start from a declared launch and say so on their card.

Why a tool and not a robot. The point is that the cause is visible and its program is known, so the intervention branch ("push harder from 0.5 s") is a physical change in the scene rather than a hidden velocity edit. The joints of an arm are not something the world model should learn: the tool enters the scene graph as a controlled object with its command, carries no loss of its own, and the arm's motion is never a prediction target. Mid-rollout force changes are exactly the commands a planner built on the model will issue.

5. Events, frame tags and checks↑ contents

Every engine run stores, besides the 120 Hz states, the contact episodes with their impulses at solver resolution (which pair, first contact, peak impulse and where), and a timed event list: comes to rest, topples, container entry and exit, intervention and release markers, gravity and fan changes, rebound and spring compression peaks. The same run passes the physics screens (penetration at most 5 mm, energy gain bounded by the recorded external work, inside the room, unit quaternions) and the family's expectation: the actor moves, the declared pairs touch, the stated stages touch in order, the tower tips, the ball ends inside the box. The branch checks of section 2 run on top. A failed check stays in the record as a valid negative and is shown on the dashboard, never silently dropped.

Why events with evidence. The planned event head and verbalizer (D1 to D4 on the World model tab) need a fixed event extractor shared by predictions and PhysX, so that "the ball never reached the box" is a comparison of two event lists, not a free-text guess. Timed events are also what frame-level question answering is grounded on: every answer below cites the state indices it was measured from. Stage order gives chains a label that a loss can use directly.

6. Questions↑ contents

Each family declares which question kinds apply: count and order of toppled objects, first tip time, ends inside, rebound height, bounce count, travel distance, greatest height, time to rest, spring compression, oscillation count, maximum rotation, contact order, direction after impact, spread, plus mass, energy, speed and contact time. The answer to each is measured from the saved states and contact reports of that run and shown with its evidence frames. The factual record also carries comparative questions across the bundle ("if the material were rubber, does the ball travel more, less or about the same?"), answered from the two saved runs. Qwen (a 27B checkpoint, run locally) is used for wording only: it rewords measured questions in natural language and may propose a typed question whose answer the simulator then measures. On the round-2 scenes it returned 83 valid rewordings and 26 proposals, 25 of a valid type; nothing numerical ever comes from the language model.

Why this split. Sim2Reason shows a simulator can grade a reasoner's physics answers for reinforcement learning, and CLEVRER shows the four question types worth asking (descriptive, explanatory, predictive, counterfactual). Measured answers make every question gradeable without a human and without trusting the language model; the comparative questions are the training signal for the counterfactual question of the world model, and the typed proposals let the question set grow without letting the model invent events.

7. Rooms, camera and renders↑ contents

Each family runs in one of fourteen whole ScanNet++ training rooms, two per type (office, apartment, bedroom, kitchen, living room, classroom, conference room). The scanned layout is transferred rigidly behind a reserved foreground zone; furniture is fitted with ReplicaCAD assets, small items are seated on the furniture below them, whiteboards and screens hang on the nearest wall, and anything that would enter the zone or leave the shell is dropped. The rooms keep 19 to 40 objects each, all with static colliders. The camera stays inside the room, below the ceiling, and walks forward to the closest distance that frames the recorded motion.

Why real rooms, and why renders are optional. The video models, the digital twins and the final use case all live in real rooms; training the state model in rooms with the same clutter, supports and wall items as the scans keeps the gap between training layouts and twins small, and the clutter is a genuine physical obstacle. The training payload remains the states and events: RGB, depth, flow and masks are rendered on demand from saved states (shown on the dashboard for four families) and are not part of the record count.

8. Durations and sampling rate↑ contents

Horizons are set per family from measured time to rest and last task event over every branch: 4 s for drops, sliding and spinning, 5 to 6 s for most, 7 s for the pendulum, tower and box chain, 8 s for pendulums, springs, chains and orbits. Nothing runs longer than 8 s, and no branch has an event after its horizon. The solver steps at 1920 Hz and states are saved at 120 Hz: subsampling the same runs to 30 Hz moved the stored position up to 7.5 cm around impacts in 14 families, 60 Hz up to 3.1 cm.

Why 120 Hz and not the first dataset's 30 Hz. At 30 Hz a body at 3 m/s moves 10 cm between frames, more than most of our objects are long, so an impact falls between two frames: the pre-impact and post-impact states are a frame apart with the contact unobserved, the position jumps and the velocity flips within one step, and a model has to infer the collision from positions alone. At 120 Hz the same body moves 2.5 cm per frame and the impact is bracketed within 8 ms. The measurement above (7.5 cm of stored displacement around impacts at 30 Hz, 3.1 cm at 60 Hz, in 14 families) is the same effect on our own runs. The rate is also what a learned replacement for a game's physics engine would need: engines step their physics at a fixed 50 to 120 Hz, independently of the frame rate at which they render, and the renderer interpolates between physics states. Interpolation is only valid between two states that do not straddle a contact, which is why engines step fast or substep around collisions, and why a learned stepper has to run at the engine's rate rather than the display's. States saved at 120 Hz train and test steppers at 120, 60, 40 or 30 Hz by taking every n-th frame, and let the timing of an impact be checked to 8 ms; 30 Hz states cannot be upsampled without inventing the dynamics in between. The data rate is the ceiling; the model's own step is a design choice below it, since a 120 Hz autoregressive model takes four times the steps per second of a 30 Hz one and compounds its error accordingly.

What this means for training. The current predictor works on a 3 s window at 30 Hz (90 frames in 15 patches), with padding masks for shorter clips; a longer record is consumed as chained 3 s blocks, the way the step-wise V5 model already chains blocks, and 120 Hz states subsample to any of those rates without losing the impact frames.

9c. New families (pilot): circular motion, rolling and spinning↑ contents

Four families added on 3 November 2026: two in which a surface, not a field, supplies the centripetal force, and two in which the spread of mass, not its amount, decides the motion. These are their pilots (the first on 3 November, the second and third on 4 November after fixes): 47 roots (reference rigs and randomized draws), one action each, every branch; not yet part of the dataset. Each family's representative clips are with the other families', in sections 3b and 9b; here are the pilots' findings and measurements.

Ball rounds a curved wall and leaves along the tangent. A piston launches a ball along a straight lead-in into a circular wall. The wall pushes the ball toward the centre, which is what turns it; where the wall ends nothing pushes any more, so the ball leaves along the tangent in a straight line and hits a block standing on that line. Removing the wall's end piece, at the start or mid-run, moves the exit and the ball misses the block. Varied per root: wall radius 0.38 to 0.55 m, arc 190 to 240 degrees, end piece 45 to 65 degrees, block distance, wall friction, push force, ball size, shape and material.

Ball orbits and spirals down a funnel. A ball launched along the slope of a funnel orbits its axis: the sloped surface pushes it inward. Losing energy, it spirals down and strikes a ball resting in the central dish. Three profiles give three orbit laws: a gravity well (like a planet, faster lower down), a cone (slower lower down) and a paraboloid bowl (one period at every radius). Varied per root: profile, rim radius, depth, launch radius and launch speed (70 to 100 percent of the rolling-sphere orbit speed), ball mass and material. First pilot (3 November): the cone and the bowl launched near orbit speed still circle 14 to 18 cm out after 8 s (the well arrives in about 4 s). Second pilot (4 November): launched at 45 to 60 percent (cone) and 35 to 50 percent (bowl) of orbit speed, the ball swings inward on its first lap; the cones reach the bottom in 1.2 to 4.9 s and strike the resting ball. The bowl at 40 percent reaches the bottom at 6.5 s but passes beside the resting ball, a valid negative. Three random roots arrived and struck the ball yet missed the travel check, then 0.5 m (their farthest point from the start was 0.43 to 0.49 m); it is 0.25 m from now on.

Rolling race: a ball, a cylinder and a hollow tube down one ramp. A solid ball, a solid cylinder and a thin-walled tube are released together from one line across a ramp, each in its own lane. Rolling without slipping, each accelerates at g sin(angle) / (1 + I / m R^2): 5/7, 2/3 and about 1/2 of g sin, so the ball wins and the tube comes last whatever their masses and radii, which are drawn independently. Varied per root: ramp angle 6 to 15 degrees, ramp length, each racer's radius and mass, the lane order. First pilot (3 November): the cylinder and the third racer, then a hoop, finish almost together (2 of 7 roots in the expected order). They were convex prisms: PhysX cooks a hull to at most 64 vertices, so the 48-sided cylinder rolled as a 32-sided one, and a faceted roller loses energy at every edge and levels off. Second pilot (4 November): the cylinder and a thin-walled tube roll on exact capsules, the drawn shape only shown, with its inertia written on the body. All 6 roots finish in the expected order, and the arrival-time ratios match the rolling accelerations to within 0.3 percent (cylinder / ball 1.034 to 1.037 against 1.035).

Spinning rod: masses pulled inward make it spin faster. A rod spins freely on a vertical axle; two masses ride on it on sliding guides, held by springs toward rest points near the axle. Started near the ends, the masses are pulled in and the rod spins faster: nothing twists the axle, so the angular momentum I omega stays constant while the moment of inertia I falls. Varied per root: spin rate, rod length and mass, slider mass, spring stiffness and damping, start and rest radii. Counterfactual (4 November): the masses are not mounted. Started co-rotating on the rod, a loose mass slides outward while omega^2 r exceeds mu g (plastic on metal: about 1 m/s^2) and flies off the end; the rod slows as the masses move out, and a grippier mass (the rubber one of the paired counterfactual) can stop short of the end. Third pilot (4 November): loose plastic masses leave the rod 0.22 to 0.32 s after release in five of six roots; the rubber mass leaves later (0.27 to 0.69 s) or stops short of the end. In the sixth root the variant had drawn a rubber rod: friction can hold up to 2.9 to 4.2 m/s^2 against a pull of 2.77 m/s^2, and neither mass slides, as the measured condition says. 10. Why plausible simulation from a given setup is hard↑ contents

Every family here starts from an ordinary setup: a few objects, a piece of furniture, a floor or a slanted surface, and one cause. Getting a rigid-body engine to produce motion that a physicist would accept from that setup is not a matter of pressing play. The table lists what actually broke on the way to 52 verified families and a measured shape vocabulary, how it was found, and what it took. None of it was visible in a rendered clip at a glance; all of it was visible in the saved states.

DifficultyWhat it looked likeHow it was caught, and the fix
Seating a body exactlyA swapped cube on the 16-degree ramp was aligned to the slope, but its gripper was not; the rigid grasp dragged the cube flat (16 degrees of twist, 9 centimetres of lift) before it ever rolled. A bench hung in place of a pendulum bob kept the sphere's bottom height, so its 1.1 m rope started 8 centimetres over-stretched and the solver flung bob and gripper through 111 degrees in the first tenth of a second.Found by the sweep's hold-quality checks (a held object must not twist, a released one must move) and energy screen, read on the saved trajectories; fixed by seating the gripper in the payload's frame and hanging swapped bodies by their centre.
Holding without a robotA gripper is a rigid grasp with a stroke-matched force and a wrist hold. Re-seating it after an unrelated swap moved it from the end of a rotating beam to its pivot; at release the freed gripper braked the beam (38 degrees before release, 1 degree after; the recipe gives 180). A sphere rolls 16 degrees inside a grasp whose limit was written for cubes.Every candidate of every family failed the same way, which is the signature of a code path rather than of physics; the reseat now only acts on the gripped body and keeps custom grasp points.
Constraints are softPhysX ropes stretch under load: 11 to 13 millimetres on a ten-link chain at 16 solver iterations, 5 millimetres on a 0.8 m rope at the moment a bob hits a tower even at 128 iterations. A fixed 2 mm gate would reject a family a viewer sees nothing wrong with; no gate would pass visibly elastic links.A gate relative to rope length (1 percent, at least 2 mm) applied at the saved states, the chain's iterations raised, the measurement stored in every record as a validity flag separate from the outcome.
Penetration scales with size and speedAt 1,920 Hz a small object sinks about 5 mm into a surface at impact; a 0.9 m chair may sink 3 percent of its extent, which would be 27 mm. Eighteen cargo shapes simply do not fit the catapult's arm tip and start inside it.Start-up overlaps are rejected on the CPU before any simulation; the dynamic allowance is 5 mm or 3 percent, capped at 15 mm; candidates that cannot fit stay out of the pool rather than out of the data after the fact.
Energy bookkeepingThe audit's potential energy used the body origin. A barrel or bench whose origin sits at its base reads as gaining energy when it tips, and 19 of 158 valid records in one shard were wrongly excluded for it. Angular springs store energy the audit never counted.Potential energy at the centre of mass, the energy screen marked unavailable for angular-spring rigs, and a re-evaluation of every saved record without re-simulating.
Chains are brittle, and order is a claimReplace the pushed peg of the branching dominoes with anything else and the chain rarely starts; replace the launched ball of the launcher and tower and the tower rarely falls. The check that the chain's stages happen in order treated the two arms of a branching chain as one sequence, so a chain whose right arm fell first was labelled a failure.Stages are now independent chains; negatives are kept as data but capped at a fifth of a family's draws so a chain family stays about chains.
Randomization versus intentFourteen families whose cause is letting go of a body received three identical action programs, because only forces were re-drawn. Solid shapes swapped at equal volume were exact, hollow vessels carried a third of the volume while the record said equal, a peg carried three times the box it replaced.Each family now varies the dimension it has (height, angle, position on the ramp, tug, thrust); identical programs are detected and not simulated; volumes are true and the contract per shape is written down.
Measuring what the question asks"How high does it rise after the first impact" took the lowest point of the whole clip, so a later bounce could answer for the first; the peak height of a tumbling block was read at the frame of the highest origin, not of the highest bottom.Windows and quantities are now defined in world space per frame, with the definition stored next to the answer.
The simulator itselfKit keeps about a quarter of a gigabyte per simulated root; three workers filled a 96 GB node in four hours and were killed mid-root. A worker killed that way looked finished to a naive resume.Workers run as chains of short lives on resumable state, every life leaves a receipt, and a run contract pins code and inputs so a resumed run cannot silently mix conditions.
A recipe can be wrong by millimetresThe diving board's six segments hinge at their shared edges. Each anchor was placed 3 mm inside its own segment, so every hinge started 6 mm apart and the solver pulled the board together at the first step, in all 211 planned records. The clips looked right and every screen passed.Found by a reviewer running the anchor check on the authored pose of every planned manifest. Both anchors now sit at the shared edge, and the same check runs before every simulation.
Mass ratios and the solverThe gripper and the piston are 0.08 kg tools. Randomized densities make a 29 kg metal ball, and a stroke-matched push of 176 N through that tool sank it 6.5 mm into the ball at 16 solver iterations; a 5 kg slab on a net of 20 g tiles pushed two tiles 8.5 mm into each other.The screens caught both. Payloads above 5 kg and forces above 50 N now run at 64 iterations, the setting the heavy-furniture families already needed at 180 to 250 N; the record carries the rule.
Constraints that hold at rest and drift in motionThe chain of ten beads on distance joints was exact while hanging still (0.00 mm) and drifted as it swung: 0.9 mm after one second, 8 mm after six, the same at 16 and 128 solver iterations. More iterations cannot hold ten serial constraints under a whipping motion.The beads now carry thin rods and hang from spherical joints at the bead above, so link lengths are exact by construction; the measured anchor error over a whole clip is 0.01 mm.
The free run before contactA piston's force is matched to its payload's mass. Scaled fifty-fold for a 29 kg ball, it accelerated the 0.08 kg tool across its 65 mm approach gap to 17 m/s; the states show the tool jumping 7 cm in one frame and sinking 6.5 mm at the impact, while the sustained push afterwards was fine.The approach gap shrinks with the force ratio, so the tool meets the payload at the recipe's contact speed; 15 and 29 kg pushes are now valid.
What a prop should weighGiving props the density of their material made a 29 kg metal ball and a 156 kg crate; the recipes' props are hollow in reality, and the median randomized prop was 15 times its authored mass. A 5 kg slab on a net of 20 g tiles is a regime the coarse net was never built for.A prop stays within max(5 kg, twice its authored mass), and rigs carry a measured load rating. The heavier regime was qualified first, so the lighter one is inside it.
A rule that is right for forces is wrong for velocitiesWhen a slot object takes its material's mass (a 0.15 kg ball becomes 2.9 kg of rubber), a push force on it should grow with the mass so the recipe still moves it. The same rule, applied to a declared launch, multiplied a thrown chair's velocity by the mass ratio of the ball on it: 16.5 m/s, the ball through the wall. Two other roots launched 5.7 and 2.3 times too fast and passed every screen, because a fast launch is not an invalid simulation.Found by reading the one record that did fail (the ball ended 31 m outside the room) and tracing the number back to the knob. Forces follow the mass of the body they act on; declared velocities never follow a mass.
A counterfactual can leave the regime"The same release with a ball twice as heavy" doubled a 12 kg ball that was already at the gripper's capacity. Before the programmed force even starts, the gripper carries the ball's weight through the grasp: 236 N on a 0.08 kg tool, which the solver could not transmit. The tool separated 18 cm in 0.2 s and 6.8 J appeared from nowhere.The energy screen flagged it. The counterfactual now stays within the gripper's capacity (halving the mass instead, and saying so) and the hold carries the new weight, as it does for randomized masses.
A correction undone by a later editThe density knob shortened the piston's approach so the tool meets a heavier payload at the recipe's speed, and the record said so. The shape swap that runs later re-seated the tool at the default 65 mm, in 455 planned records, and every screen and test passed because each step was right on its own.The gap is a property of the tool program, written when the scene is built and read by every re-seat; the dry run measures it on every planned branch, and an end-to-end test builds whole roots with every knob.
A rating on one path and not the otherThe bridge's 2 kg rating capped the density draw but not the counterfactual that doubles the load: 27 planned records at 4 kg, a regime the probe had just shown to fail.One rule for both paths: every mass edit of a rig's load respects the rating.
Shape, not mass aloneThe net held spheres from 1 to 5 kg; then a 2 kg flat book sank 14 mm into a tile, and after more solver iterations a small 2 kg cube still dents one by 7 mm. A rating in kilograms says nothing about edges and footprints.The pool row is qualified shape by shape at the rating (four shapes removed), and the remaining edge case stays a flagged record rather than a rule invented without evidence.
Names follow recipesThe questions called a book-shaped payload "the ball" because names came from the family and the object id, written before shapes were randomized.The noun follows the final shape; roles keep their names.
Rooms emptier than the scansThe layouts keep the strip in front of the furniture clear so no recipe collides with the room, and keep 19 to 40 of the 131 to 284 objects of each scanned room. A model that reads the nearest objects never meets a near, idle one in training and meets little else in a scanned twin.Static clutter that cannot have touched the motion is a fact about the scene, not the physics: it is drawn after the run from the recorded motion and merged at load time, at the density of the scans if wanted.
Why this is hard for people as well. Each row is a judgment a person makes by hand when building one scene in a simulator: where exactly a mug sits on a slanted shelf, how firmly a hand must hold a ball so that it neither slips nor crushes, how much a rope may stretch before the eye objects, how deep an impact may go at a given frame rate. Fifty-six recipes times thousands of randomized variants turn every such judgment into a measured rule with a threshold, and every threshold can be wrong in both directions: too tight and valid data is thrown away, too loose and broken physics enters the training set. Most of the thresholds above were wrong in one direction the first time. What made the fixes tractable was that every record keeps its saved states, contact timings and receipts, so a wrong rule could be located in minutes and re-evaluated on thousands of records without a simulator.
