How each method looks next to the physics engine in the clips on the State predictors tabs, rated by eye with the method's name and error hidden. These numbers come from the visuals only. Updated 06 November 2026, 16:00 UTC.

In short

  1. Best on the simple set: non-block regression (right on 39 of 50 clips) and masked regression, forward only (38 of 50). Neither has a clip rated not plausible. They visibly shake in 2 and 3 of 50.
  2. Non-block flow is next: 32 of 50 right, none implausible, none visibly shaking. It is the calmest of the methods that mostly get the motion right.
  3. Asking one model all the questions costs looks: masked regression falls to 32 of 50 (5 implausible, 10 shaking), masked flow from 27 to 20 of 50.
  4. Regression chains: chain 5 is right on 30 of 50, chain 10 on 26, chain 15 on 25, chain 3 on 24. Flow chain 5 is the shakiest: 20 of 50 right, 14 wrong, 10 implausible, 15 shaking, and something flies out of view in 7.
  5. Furniture separates the methods most: non-block regression and masked regression (forward only) are right on 6 of 7, regression chains on 0 or 1 of 7. Most visible shake is in these scenes: table topple, chair push, cabinet tip, crate drop.
  6. The first single-block models look worst, along with diffusion: regression 100M is right on 1 of 50, flow 100M on 9, non-block diffusion on 6 (27 wrong, 12 implausible).
  7. Easiest: falls and mechanisms, right for the two best methods on every clip. Hardest: hits, chain reactions and stacks, where the two best methods are right on 50-67% and partly right on the rest. Grains: 3 of 21 methods look right.

How this was rated · the rating scales, the control and how far to trust the counts
  • What was rated: the clips on the State predictors and State predictors on V2 tabs. Each method's tile next to the physics engine: five moments of the clip, plus where things moved, where the picture trembles and how much it changes over time, all taken from the published video.
  • Hidden from the raters: the method (a random code per clip) and the tile's error number.
  • Scales: right = same objects, same outcome, same timing; partly = same kind of event, visibly off; wrong = a different outcome. Not plausible = an obvious violation seen on its own: continuous shaking, passing through things, hovering, parts coming apart, motion with no cause.
  • Visibly shakes: the rater saw shaking, or the published video's pixels show it: the tile's picture changes faster than about 3 times a second at least twice as much as the physics engine's tile in the same clip. On the clips checked by eye, the tiles this marks show jagged motion paths and the others smooth ones. Shaking smaller than a pixel of these tiles does not show here, so the Physical plausibility block of the State predictors tab, which works on the predicted positions, finds more of it.
  • Control: the physics engine was slipped in under a code in every clip. It was rated right in 52 of 52 clips.
  • How easy to judge: 15 clips clear, 35 partly, 2 too small or dark (left out). The sheets are small: objects of a few pixels can hide small errors.
  • Limits: AI raters looked at frames and motion maps, not the playing video, and one or two clips per family is a small sample, so counts within a kind are rough.

Check after rating, not used in any number on this page: on the simple set the tiles' hidden errors rise with the ratings, with a median of 0.085 m for right, 0.28 m for partly and 0.51 m for wrong. The ratings also catch what the error misses: in trampoline, tiles with about 0.05 m error are rated wrong because the bowl passes through the table.
