Digital twins from photographs

A real kitchen counter, photographed, turned into objects that the physics simulator can move

Why digital twins, and why go beyond them?

Physics on a real scene is hard today. A physics engine needs every object as a 3D shape with a collision body, a mass and a friction, so a real scene must first be rebuilt as a digital twin: objects outlined in photographs, completed into full 3D shapes, placed faithfully in the simulator and given physical parameters, before PhysX can move anything, and whatever it predicts has to be carried back to the real scene. This page follows that route on a real kitchen counter.

The aim is to need no twin at all. The world model learns physics in simulation; with an efficient way to read a scene graph (objects, shapes, poses, materials) from photographs taken from a few viewpoints, it can predict directly from that scene graph, an instruction and an action, and the motion can be watched in the real scene itself, with nothing ported into a simulator and back. The twin stays useful as a yardstick: it gives PhysX's answer on a real scene's geometry, to check the world model against.

1. Abstract

The benchmark's rooms are built by hand. A digital twin starts instead from photographs of a real scene and turns the objects in them into rigid bodies the simulator can push, drop and throw. The example is the Mip-NeRF 360 counter, with three movable objects: a black tin, a red tin and a coconut-oil jar. Everything else on the counter stays fixed.

3. How the twin is built

Five steps, from photographs to a scene the simulator can run.

Photographs and cameras

The capture has 240 photographs with known camera positions, and a dense 3D point cloud of the counter built from them. Seven of the photographs are used to outline the objects. Click one to see where its camera stood.

The reconstructed counter (objects removed) and the real camera positions.

From an outline to a complete object

SAM2 outlines each object from a few clicks placed by hand. SAM 3D Objects turns one photograph and its outline into a complete 3D shape, including the sides no camera saw, which are a guess. Each shape is then scaled and placed to match the 3D points the cameras saw, to about 1.6 mm; the two tins are also matched to their outlines in other photographs.

The reconstructed shapes, simplified for the browser.

4. What the simulator gets for each object

PropertyValueWhere it comes from
Shape and colourreconstructed meshone photograph per object; hidden sides and much of the colour are generated
Sizemetric scaleassumed: the jar is 9 cm across
Collision shapeconvex hull with a flat basederived from the mesh
Mass0.45 kg per tin, 0.55 kg for the jarassumed
Friction and bouncefriction 0.6 / 0.5, bounce 0.05assumed

The photographs fix shape and position. Mass, friction and bounce cannot be seen and are reasonable guesses. Known limits: the generated colours are less faithful than the photographs (label text is not readable), and the reconstructed surroundings have holes.

5. What comes next

Find the objects automatically

Today the three objects were picked by hand. A detector such as LEDGER could propose objects and keep their identities across photographs.

Predict before simulating

The twin is stored in the same scene format as the benchmark rooms, so the world model could predict the outcome of an action, with PhysX checking the chosen one.

Show the motion in the photographs

Moving the captured appearance with the simulated poses (as in PhysGaussian) would render the result in the real scene. Not built yet.
