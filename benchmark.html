Can a language model edit a physical scene?

GPT-5.4 and Qwen3-VL-30B Thinking rearrange furniture and launch objects in simulated rooms; the PhysX simulator judges every answer

Every number, picture and video below is a real record of the benchmark. Back to the landing page.

In plain words

  1. The task. A simulated living room with 24 to 34 objects and an instruction in everyday language: "bring the red chair closer to the table", "roll the red ball so that it hits the blue ball within one and a half seconds", "toss the red ball over the rim of the blue open box".
  2. What the model gets. Two rendered views of the room and the scene as a list of objects with their positions, sizes, masses and friction, plus the rules by which the answer will be checked. One call, no tools, no second try.
  3. What it returns. For a static edit: the new position and orientation of each object it moves. For a launch task: one velocity for one object (written as a small program in the main runs, or directly as numbers in the newest run).
  4. How it is judged. The answer is applied in the PhysX physics engine exactly as sent. The simulator then measures what the instruction asked for (distances, angles, contacts, where things end up, whether they came to rest) and whether anything overlaps or leaves the room. A separate reviewer AI looks at the rendered result and comments; its opinion is recorded but can never overturn a measurement.

Results at a glance

Goal met means every measured check passed in the simulator. An answer the model could not produce in the required form counts as a failure and is listed separately. Random answers are launches drawn uniformly inside the allowed range, or for static edits a random placement of the object on its surface; their pass rate tells how much of a task is luck.

What stands out. GPT-5.4 meets the goal in most tasks of every finished set, including the throws over a barrier or box rim, where a random launch almost never works. Qwen3-VL-30B Thinking solves fewer than half of the tasks in every set; on the launch tasks a sizeable share of its program answers never produced a usable launch at all. Asked for the velocity directly as numbers instead of a program, the Qwen model does better (last complete row of the table once the run finishes), so part of its gap was the programming, not the physics. The reviewer AI agreed with the simulator on 99 percent of the attempts it saw.

How hard is a task?

We grade difficulty by luck: for every task we draw random answers from its own allowed range, run them through the same checks, and count the passes. A task that random answers pass half the time is easy; one they almost never pass is hard. Every static edit and every throw is hard by this measure; the floor launches spread across all three tiers.
