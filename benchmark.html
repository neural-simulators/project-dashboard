Can a language model edit a physical scene?

GPT-5.4 and Qwen3-VL-30B Thinking rearrange furniture and launch objects in simulated rooms; the PhysX simulator judges every answer

Every number, picture and video below is a real record of the benchmark. Back to the landing page.

Why this benchmark?

Before a language model can be trusted to plan physical actions, we need to know whether it understands space and motion. Static edits test spatial reasoning: where objects must go and whether they stay put. Launches and throws test dynamics: how hard and in which direction to push so that something specific happens. Composite tasks need several launches at once, and every one of them has to work.

The model answers once; PhysX executes the answer exactly and measures whether the instruction holds. Random answers drawn from each task's allowed range show how much of a task is luck.

  • 14 roomsfurnished, 24 to 34 objects each
  • 2 modelsGPT-5.4 and Qwen3-VL-30B Thinking
  • PhysXthe only judge

Results

Goal met means every check the simulator measured passed. An answer the model could not produce in the required form counts as a failure. Random answers are drawn uniformly from each task's allowed range; their pass rate shows how much of a task is luck.

  • GPT-5.4 meets the goal in 87 to 95 percent of the tasks in every set, including the throws over a barrier or box rim, where a random launch works about one time in ten.
  • Qwen3-VL-30B Thinking meets the goal in 31 to 40 percent of the tasks. On launches, about one answer in five never became a usable launch; the launches it did produce were mostly too weak or aimed too flat.
  • Asking for the velocity as numbers instead of a program lifts Qwen from 26 to 38 of the same 80 launch tasks and GPT-5.4 from 72 to 76, so programming explains part of Qwen's gap but not most of it.
  • Each model also forecast where its own launch would end. GPT-5.4 is off by a median of 4 cm on the floor launches and 15 cm on the throws; Qwen by 38 cm and 87 cm.
  • The reviewer AI, which looks at the renders, agreed with the simulator on 377 of 379 attempts it reviewed.

How hard is a task? We draw random answers from each task's allowed range, run them through the same checks and count the passes. A task that random answers pass half the time is easy; one they almost never pass is hard. Every static edit and every throw is hard by this measure; the floor launches spread across the three tiers.

Composite tasks: several objects at once

The newest and hardest set: knock a chair over, shove the dining table half a metre with the cups still on it, and throw the ball over the barrier, all in one answer with one velocity per object. Every requirement must hold at once, and the launched objects can disturb each other. Six task families in fourteen rooms.

What goes wrong

  • GPT-5.4 spins chairs instead of pushing them. 43 of its 49 failures have one cause: a chair that does not tip over. Its own stated reasoning treats the chair as a free body: it computes the tipping angle and gives the chair a strong spin (about 7 rad/s) with a deliberately gentle push (about 0.35 m/s), so that the chair stays near its spot and away from the table. In the simulator a chair standing on its legs does not spin freely: the floor stops its feet, and most of the spin is lost the moment it is launched. What tips a chair over is a push, which the friction at its feet turns into a fall. No random launch with a push under 0.6 m/s toppled a chair, whatever the spin. The same habit made it fail the single-object "topple the stool" tasks.
  • Everything else GPT-5.4 gets right. Table shoves, tosses into the box, throws over the barrier and wall bounces almost always succeed (it meets 13 of 14 "shove the table and toss the ball into the box" tasks, against 11 percent for random answers). The rare other misses are near-misses: a table that slides 0.245 m where 0.25 m is required, a ball launched so gently that it ends up resting against the wall instead of rolling back.
  • Qwen3-VL-30B Thinking fails before the physics starts. 48 of its 84 answers are rejected, 47 of them because they give the ball or the box a spin although the prompt allows them none. Where the table must slide "about half a metre", it gives it a speed of 0.5 m/s in 26 of 42 answers, as if speed were distance; the table then slides about 4 cm. Some answers leave the chair without any motion, others throw it far out of its area.
