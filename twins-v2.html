Twins v2

Digital twins of real rooms from a few photographs per object, with open models, scored by GATOR's protocol and simulated in Isaac Sim.

Can open models turn a few photographs per object into digital twins of real rooms that a physics simulator can use?

Yes, for the scenes tested: GATOR's three ScanNet++ benchmark rooms, three more ScanNet++ rooms (a living room, a bedroom, a kitchen) and two outdoor scenes from Mip-NeRF 360, where only photographs exist (section 3). From 3 to 10 photographs per object, SAM 3D Objects and GATOR's placement step give twins whose position and orientation are more accurate than GATOR's on its own rooms (pose error 0.035 against 0.041); their shape is still less accurate (shape error 0.056 against 0.031). After two physics steps of our own, grounding and a layout step, 87 of the 88 indoor twins and 4 of the 4 outdoor twins stand when every twin of a scene is released together in Isaac Sim, and they respond to a push, a throw or a slide like furniture. The final pipeline uses no agent.

1. How we got here↑ contents

The figure reads left to right, top row first. The top row is about each object on its own. GATOR's route with the open Pixal3D weights placed objects well but produced chairs in fragments, which fall over in the simulator (11 of 51 stand); the repair agent GATOR uses (here Claude editing the mesh in Blender) could not mend fragments. Mundo's box proxies always stand but have no shape, so only one idea survived: a twin that falls on its own gets its bounding box as collider. Switching the generator to SAM 3D Objects was the turning point: whole meshes, the best pose error in the comparison, and 45 of 51 twins stand alone. The agent on SAM 3D meshes kept only edits that extend legs to the floor.

The bottom row is about whole rooms. Released together as placed, the twins of a room overlap each other by a few centimetres and PhysX shoots them apart. A layout step that lets PhysX push them apart slowly helped, but it keeps only sideways moves, and most overlaps were stacked: legless tables had been lowered onto the chairs beneath them. Grounding fixed the cause: every twin keeps the height its placement estimated, and missing legs are added down to the floor (the agent's fix, now automatic and free). With a sideways nudge for the overlaps left, 50 of 51 twins stand when their room settles together.

2. The final pipeline↑ contents

Blue: the reconstruction of each object from its photographs. Amber: the physics that turns a room of placed meshes into a room that simulates.

Reconstruction, per object (blue)

  1. Photographs. 3 to 10 of the room capture's DSLR photographs that show the object: what anyone with a camera has.
  2. Masks. SAM 2.1 cuts the object out of each photograph, prompted with the box and a few points of the dataset's annotation, as GATOR does.
  3. Cameras. The capture's own poses. With poses VGGT estimates from the photographs alone, the pose error only rises from 0.035 to 0.037 on GATOR's rooms.
  4. SAM 3D Objects. Each masked photograph becomes a coloured mesh with a pose in that photograph's camera, so one object has several candidates.
  5. Best candidate. The mesh whose outline agrees best with the masks in all photographs is kept.
  6. Placement. GATOR's step shifts and scales the mesh, and turns it slightly, until its outlines match the masks and its surface matches VGGT's 3D points.

Physics, per room (amber)

  1. Collider. CoACD splits each mesh into up to 32 convex pieces, the shapes a physics engine can collide; the mass comes from the category.
  2. Grounding. Every twin keeps the height its placement estimated. Where the generator left out legs, four grey legs (chairs, tables) or a plinth (cabinets, shelves, bins) reach down to the floor; a mesh that pokes into the floor is cut at it; an object on furniture, such as a monitor on a desk, rests on that twin.
  3. Alone in Isaac. Each twin on its own on a floor for 6 s: 49 of 51 stand in GATOR's rooms, 32 of 37 in the three more rooms, 3 of 4 outdoors. One that tips over gets its bounding box as collider instead (Mundo's rule), unless it leans on a neighbour in the photographs: a twin that touches another sideways and, alone, falls towards it (the bicycle against the bench) keeps its own shape, and if it still falls when the room settles it gets the box too and the room runs again.
  4. Layout. Twins placed one by one can overlap their neighbours by a few centimetres. A short PhysX run with gravity off pushes them apart slowly, then the lighter twin of any pair still overlapping is moved sideways by the smallest step that clears it, so the room starts with no overlap.
  5. Room in Isaac. Every twin of the room together on the floor, then a push, a throw or a slide.
Where ground truth enters. The annotation's boxes and points prompt the masks (as in GATOR), the capture's camera poses are used in the main condition, and the floor plane sits at the laser scan's floor height. In pictures and clips the scan is only the backdrop: it is not a collider and walls are not simulated. The scanned objects are used only to score. Outdoors none of this exists; section 3 says what replaces each.
Where an agent is used. Nowhere in the final pipeline. The only agent tried is GATOR's Blender repair agent (Claude Sonnet): 2 of 14 repairs kept on Pixal3D meshes, 3 of 14 on SAM 3D meshes, about $5 of API use; every kept repair extended legs to the floor, which grounding now does for every twin without a model call (appendix B).

3. From photographs alone: what was hard and what got us through↑ contents

A simulator needs more than a shape from each object: every object at its true size, in the right place, the right way up, on a ground it can collide with. A shape generator gives little of that. Indoors the dataset hands us most of it; outdoors we had to recover each piece from the photographs.

What a ScanNet++ room hands you

  • DSLR photographs with cameras already in metres. The cameras are in the laser scan's frame, so a twin placed with them comes out at true size; nothing has to be estimated. (With cameras VGGT estimates from the photographs alone, the pose error on GATOR's rooms rises only from 0.035 to 0.037.)
  • A laser scan of the whole room: a coloured mesh, level and in metres. We read the floor height from it, use it to pick the photographs that actually see each object (a z-buffer of its vertices), and draw it behind the twins. Depth for any photograph could be rendered from it; the reconstruction does not read it.
  • A 3D annotation on the scan: every object labelled, instance by instance, which also says which objects exist. It is not a set of masks: ScanNet++ has no object masks for its photographs. Projected into a photograph it gives a rough outline, and SAM 2.1 makes the mask from that outline's box and points and is checked against it.
  • A reference mesh of each object, cut out of the scan (by DP-Recon in GATOR's rooms, by the annotation in the three others). This is ground truth and is used only to score. We could have fed it to the pipeline, for true size, pose or complete shape; we never did, because the scores would then mean nothing.
  • iPhone RGB-D video of the same room, which we do not use.

So the indoor pipeline is not photographs alone: its cameras, floor height, mask prompts and choice of photographs all lean on the dataset. Only the reference meshes stay out.

What an outdoor capture hands you

Mip-NeRF 360 gives 185 to 194 photographs per scene, COLMAP's cameras and COLMAP's sparse 3D points. Nothing else: no annotation, no laser scan, no unit of length, no "up", no reference meshes. The figure sets the two side by side.

What was missing, and what it took

  1. Which objects, and where each is in a photograph. Nothing names the objects, so we name them. OWLv2, an open-vocabulary detector, looks for a short text such as "a park bench" in every photograph, and its best box prompts SAM 2 for the mask. The object's 3D points are then the COLMAP points inside its mask in most of the photographs that see them, and 7 to 10 photographs per object are chosen by mask quality and viewpoint spread. The garden's small ball under the table had too few good photographs and is left out.
  2. True size. COLMAP's world has no unit. MoGe-2 predicts depth in metres in 24 photographs, and its median ratio to COLMAP's depth at COLMAP's own points gives one number per scene. The only outside check available: the garden's table top comes out 0.76 m high, and garden tables are about 0.75 m. MoGe-2's depth maps are used for this one number; the backdrop no longer reads them.
  3. Up, and the floor. COLMAP's world has no up either. The plane through most of COLMAP's points, close to the cameras' average up (within 1.4°), becomes height 0. The real ground is not a plane, which item 5 comes back to.
  4. The surroundings. A scan gives these for free. The first backdrop fused MoGe-2 depth from 24 photographs, each rescaled by one number. It looked fine in the garden and broke up in the park, with the same number of photographs: the depth maps disagree in shape, not only in scale (within one photograph the ratio to COLMAP's depth spreads by 0.14, in the garden 0.06), so each photograph laid down its own lawn, 30 cm below COLMAP's points and in 5,543 pieces. We tried the repairs in order. All ~190 photographs instead of 24 connected more of the surface but left the ground just as low (38 cm). A smooth correction per photograph, fitted to COLMAP's points, is what rescues single-photograph depth: 7 cm with MoGe-2. Apple Depth Pro was the weakest of the three models and Depth Anything 3 the best (6 cm; in the garden it needed no correction at all). Dropping depth-edge pixels removed a third of the separate pieces in the park (13,723 to 9,381). Floating fragments still remained, because a model looking at one photograph blurs a thin thing and what is behind it. COLMAP's multi-view stereo, which matches the photographs against each other instead of predicting from one, gave a single clean surface: 96% of it connected, 207 pieces, the ground within 0.3 cm of COLMAP's points. It costs about 80 minutes per scene on one GPU.
  5. A ground to stand and roll on. Indoors a flat floor is right. Outdoors a ball on a flat plane ignores the lawn's slopes, and a thin static mesh as ground let a ball fall through on this Isaac build. So the ground is made of 20 cm tiles, each a static box whose top follows a plane fitted to the lowest COLMAP points there, flat under the twins so the grounded twins stand as before, with grass or paving chosen by colour (grass softer and less bouncy). PhysX has no rolling resistance, so the football's spin is damped to stand in for the grass. COLMAP has few points on grass, and tiles with fewer than 8 points take the backdrop's height: 705 of the park's 1303 tiles did, so the broken backdrop had also sunk the park's ground. We found this while fixing the backdrop; its new surface raised the ground by 11 cm (median over the tiles), and all outdoor physics was run again. Dropped footballs: 0 of 31 fall through.
  6. An object that stands only against another. The bicycle falls when settled alone, as a real one would; it stands because it leans on the bench. The box rule of step 9 turned it into its 0.8 x 2.0 x 1.2 m bounding box, which the layout step then pushed 0.42 m off the bench . Now a twin that falls alone keeps its own shape when it touches another twin sideways and, alone, falls towards it (here: overlap 4.7 cm, direction agreement 0.97 of 1). The layout step then moves the bicycle 6 cm and it stands leaning. In GATOR's three rooms no twin meets the rule, so their results do not change. The room run has the last word. The bedroom's bed met the rule too (it touches the desk, overlap 3.8 cm, and alone it falls towards it), but the desk does not hold it up: it tilted 26° in the room. A twin that falls there gets its box after all and the room runs again, and the bed now stands.
  7. Knowing whether it worked. There are no reference meshes outdoors, so shape and pose cannot be scored at all. What can be said is what the physics shows: whether the twins stand together and how they respond. Outdoor results have that limit throughout.

How we got there outdoors

What did not work

  • Pixal3D, GATOR's route with its open weights: chairs come out in fragments, and 11 of 51 twins stand alone.
  • A repair agent (Claude editing meshes in Blender): 2 of 14 repairs kept on Pixal3D meshes, 3 of 14 on SAM 3D meshes, and every kept one only extended legs to the floor.
  • Box proxies alone (Mundo's route): they always stand (51 of 51), but they are boxes, with pose error 0.094 on GATOR's rooms, and not twins.
  • SAM 3 for the masks: no better than SAM 2.1.
  • Releasing a room as placed: 8 of 14 in office, 0 of 15 in conference room a, 5 of 22 in conference room b stand. Overlaps of a few centimetres make PhysX launch the twins apart.
  • The layout step alone: 10 of 14 in office, 9 of 15 in conference room a, 10 of 22 in conference room b stand. It keeps only sideways moves, and most overlaps were stacked: snapping each twin's lowest point to the floor had lowered legless tables onto the chairs beneath them.
  • A flat floor and a thin mesh ground outdoors, and the box rule on a leaning bicycle (items 5 and 6 above).
  • Depth from single photographs for the surroundings, even corrected and with the best model: better, but never one clean surface (item 4 above).

What worked

  • SAM 3D Objects, the turning point: whole meshes, 45 of 51 stand alone, and with the best candidate per object and GATOR's placement step a pose error of 0.035 against GATOR's 0.041.
  • Grounding plus the layout step and a nudge, each fixing what the other cannot: 13 of 14 in office, 15 of 15 in conference room a, 22 of 22 in conference room b stand.
  • A box collider only for a twin that falls alone, unless it leans on a neighbour and the room run shows that it stands: the one idea kept from the box proxies.
  • Outdoors: OWLv2 with SAM 2 for objects, MoGe-2 for the one number it is good at, a plane through COLMAP's points for gravity, terrain tiles for the ground, and COLMAP's dense stereo for the surroundings.

The combination that got us there

No single part does it. Indoors: SAM 3D Objects run on each photograph, the candidate that agrees best with all masks, GATOR's placement, CoACD colliders, grounding, the stand-alone test with a box for twins that fall (unless they lean), then the layout step and the nudge. That gives 87 of 88 twins standing together across 6 rooms. Outdoors: the same chain behind OWLv2 and SAM 2, MoGe-2's scale and the fitted ground, with the dense-stereo backdrop and terrain tiles: 4 of 4 twins standing together. The generator supplies shape and pose; only the physics steps make the result simulate; and neither would run outdoors without the pieces recovered above.

Not solved. Shape is still behind GATOR's (shape error 0.056 against 0.031). SAM 3D placed two tables visibly tilted: the living room's top by 5° and the bedroom desk's by 11°, so things slide on them; and it put the living room table's legs on top of its round top (the photographs show only the top), reaching 29 cm above it. Every measure, defined
ADD-SB
Chamfer distance between the posed twin and the scanned object, divided by the object's diameter, with no alignment: shape and placement together. ADD-SB < 0.1 is the share below 0.1.
CD, NC, F1
After a similarity ICP: mean two-way nearest-neighbour distance over the diameter; mean absolute normal agreement; harmonic mean of precision and recall at 0.02 D.
IoU2D
Outline IoU of the posed twin against the masks, averaged over the object's photographs.
Diameter D
Convex-hull diameter of the scanned object (ScanNet++) or of the artist model (HANDAL).
Stands
Tilt of the up axis below 10°, horizontal drift below 5 cm and no sinking by more than 10 cm (6 s alone, or 8 s with the whole room).
