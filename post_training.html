Post-training a 7B language model on simulator-verified scene edits

Standalone preview of the post-training blocks of the Neural Simulators page

Post-training an open model with the simulator as its teacher

Why post-train a language model?

Language models are the natural interface for instructions like "knock the red chair over" or "toss the ball into the box", and a model that also judges physics well could plan actions for a simulator or a robot. On the benchmark, GPT-5.4 meets the goal in about nine tasks out of ten. Open models, the ones anyone can run, inspect and fine-tune, do much worse: the 30B model passes a third to a half of the tasks and the 7B model almost none.

Post-training asks whether that gap can be closed with the simulator as the teacher instead of human labels. The model proposes several answers to a task, each answer is checked (geometric checks for a static edit, a full PhysX simulation for a launch), and small adapters are trained toward the answers that worked while the model's own weights stay frozen. The feedback is exact and unlimited; its cost is time, about 50 seconds per simulated launch, which a fast world model could cut to milliseconds. Every scene, answer and score below is a real record from the benchmark.

1. Abstract

The benchmark has two kinds of task. A static edit asks the model to move or turn named objects so that an instruction holds ("bring the red chair closer to the brown table"). A launch task asks for one initial velocity of one object so that something happens in the simulation ("toss the red ball over the barrier into the box"). Both are judged by deterministic checks and the PhysX simulator. GPT-5.4 passes most of them, the 30B open model a third to a half, and the 7B open models almost none.

Post-training turns those checks into a teacher. The model writes several answers to the same prompt, each answer is scored (cheap geometry checks for static edits, a full simulation for every launch), and the model is pushed toward the answers that scored better than their siblings (GRPO), or toward the better answer of a pair (DPO, the cheaper baseline). The simulator is the final judge, on held-out tasks the model never trained on.

2. Post-training in symbols

The notation of the World model tab carries over: \(G\) is the scene graph, \(a\) an action, \(\Phi\) the simulator and \(V\) a task's verdict. What is new is the language model as a policy.

\(x\)
the prompt: instruction, two pictures, the scene as data, the rules of the checks, the answer format
\(y = (y_1, \dots, y_L)\)
the answer, a sequence of tokens (JSON)
\(\pi_\theta(y \mid x) = \prod_{l} \pi_\theta(y_l \mid x, y_{\lt l})\)
the language model as a policy over answers
\(W = W_0 + BA\)
LoRA: every weight matrix \(W_0\) stays frozen and only the low-rank \(B A\) (rank 32) is trained, about 1 percent of the weights
\(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)
the model before training, the anchor for DPO
\(\psi(y)\)
the parser: the action \(a\) the answer states (new poses, or a launch velocity), or \(\bot\) when it breaks the answer contract
\(r(x, y)\)
the reward, always computed by code, never by a person or another model

The verified reward

For a launch the reward is built from the simulator's own verdict, with partial credit so that a group of failed answers can still be ranked (\(K\) checks, \(\mathbb{1}[\cdot]\) is 1 when true):

\[ r(x, y) = 0.1\,\mathbb{1}[\psi(y) \text{ parses}] + 0.2\,\mathbb{1}[\psi(y) \text{ is allowed}] + 0.4\, \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} g_k\big(\Phi(G, \psi(y))\big) + 0.3\, V\big(\Phi(G, \psi(y))\big) \]

For a static edit the simulator is replaced by geometric checks on the new poses (the requested relations, overlaps, support, tilt), which agree with PhysX's verdict on 60 of 61 stored answers: \(r = 0.05\, r_{\text{format}} + 0.10\, r_{\text{contract}} + 0.35\, r_{\text{relation}} + 0.20\, r_{\text{physical}} + 0.30\, r_{\text{pass}}\).

Three ways to train

SFT: imitate reference answers \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x, y^\star)}\, \log \pi_\theta(y^\star \mid x) \]

\(y^\star\) is the verified reference answer. The control arm: it teaches one way of answering and needs a reference for every task.

DPO: prefer the better of two own answers \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)} \log \sigma\!\Big( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)} \Big) \]

For each prompt the start model writes several answers; the best and the worst become a pair \((y_w, y_l)\) when their rewards differ by at least 0.25. \(\beta = 0.1\). Offline: the simulator scores the answers once, before training.

GRPO: learn from a group of fresh answers \[ \hat A_g = \frac{r_g - \operatorname{mean}(r_{1:G})}{\operatorname{std}(r_{1:G}) + \delta}, \qquad J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\Big[ \frac{1}{\sum_{g} |y_g|} \sum_{g=1}^{G} \sum_{l=1}^{|y_g|} \min\big(\rho_{g,l} \hat A_g,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_{g,l}, 1 \pm \epsilon)\, \hat A_g\big) \Big] - \beta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_\theta \,\Vert\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\big) \]

For every prompt the current model writes \(G\) answers \(y_1, \dots, y_G\), each is scored, and each answer's advantage is its reward relative to its own group. \(\rho_{g,l}\) is the probability ratio of token \(l\) under the new and the sampling policy. In our run \(G = 8\), two prompts per update, 100 updates, \(\beta = 0\) and one gradient step per batch, so \(\rho = 1\), the clip never binds and the update is

\[ \nabla_\theta J = \frac{1}{\sum_{g} |y_g|} \sum_{g=1}^{G} \hat A_g \sum_{l=1}^{|y_g|} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta\big(y_{g,l} \mid x, y_{g, \lt l}\big) \]

A group whose answers all score the same carries no signal (\(\hat A_g = 0\)); that was 32 percent of the groups on average. Online: every sampled launch is simulated during training.

5. DPO: learning from a better and a worse answer

The offline baseline: answers are sampled and scored once, then the model is trained to prefer the better answer of each pair. No simulator runs during training.

6. What is trained, and where the simulator sits

The 7.6 billion weights of the language model stay frozen. LoRA adds two small matrices beside every linear layer and trains only those, about one percent of the model, which is what lets a ten-thousand-token example fit one graphics card.

8. Next: a world model as the judge

GRPO needs a verdict for every answer it samples, and today each launch verdict is a PhysX simulation. The proposal is to ask the world model instead, hosted on the same machine as the language model, so that a whole group of answers is judged in one batched pass:

\[ \hat r(x, y) = 0.1\,\mathbb{1}[\psi(y) \text{ parses}] + 0.2\,\mathbb{1}[\psi(y) \text{ is allowed}] + 0.4\, \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k} \hat g_k + 0.3\, \hat p(x, y) \] \[ \hat p(x, y) = \frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^{S} V\big(Y^{(s)}\big), \qquad Y^{(s)} \sim p_\theta\big(\cdot \mid G, \psi(y)\big) \]

A generative world model gives \(S\) possible futures, so \(\hat p\) is a probability of success rather than a yes or no (\(S = 1\) for a deterministic one). DPO needs the same verdicts, only once and offline.

What accuracy is needed

Not a small distance error but the same verdicts as PhysX on the answers the policy actually writes. Two numbers decide: the agreement \(\alpha\) and the false-success rate \(\mathrm{FS}\), measured on the policy's own samples \(y \sim \pi_\theta\):

\[ \alpha = \Pr_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\big[\hat V = V\big], \qquad \mathrm{FS} = \Pr_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\big[\hat V = 1 \mid V = 0\big] \]

Random mistakes are tolerable. If every verdict were flipped independently with probability \(\eta \lt 1/2\), the expected reward would be \(\mathbb{E}[\hat r] = (1 - 2\eta)\, \mathbb{E}[r] + \eta\): answers keep their order and GRPO's signal only shrinks. Systematic false successes are not tolerable: training searches for answers the world model wrongly accepts and makes them more likely (model exploitation).

Safeguards

  • Audit. PhysX re-checks a share \(q\) of the answers in every update, in particular each group's best answer, the one GRPO reinforces most; audited answers keep PhysX's reward.
  • Doubt goes to PhysX. When the world model's samples disagree about the verdict (\(0 \lt \hat p \lt 1\)), that answer is simulated.
  • The judge keeps learning. Every audited answer becomes a new training rollout for the world model, on exactly the answers the policy now writes.
  • The final word stays with PhysX: trained models are judged by the simulator on held-out tasks, as now.

Where it stands. Not yet: in the end-to-end study the best world model recognizes 24 percent of real successes, and the toy models' predicted trajectories pass none of the benchmark's checks. The switch is gated on \(\alpha\) and \(\mathrm{FS}\) measured on policy samples. Static edits already work this way: their reward replaces the simulator with geometric checks that agree with PhysX on 60 of 61 stored answers.
