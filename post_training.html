Post-training an open model with the simulator as its teacher

Why post-train a language model?

Language models are the natural interface for instructions like "knock the red chair over" or "toss the ball into the box", and a model that also judges physics well could plan actions for a simulator or a robot. On the benchmark, GPT-5.4 meets the goal in about nine tasks out of ten. Open models, the ones anyone can run, inspect and fine-tune, do much worse: the 30B model passes a third to a half of the tasks and the 7B model almost none.

Post-training asks whether that gap can be closed with the simulator as the teacher instead of human labels. The model proposes several answers to a task, each answer is checked (geometric checks for a static edit, a full PhysX simulation for a launch), and small adapters are trained toward the answers that worked while the model's own weights stay frozen. The feedback is exact and unlimited; its cost is time, about 50 seconds per simulated launch, which a fast world model could cut to milliseconds. 1. Abstract

The benchmark has two kinds of task. A static edit asks the model to move or turn named objects so that an instruction holds ("bring the red chair closer to the brown table"). A launch task asks for one initial velocity of one object so that something happens in the simulation ("toss the red ball over the barrier into the box"). Both are judged by deterministic checks and the PhysX simulator. GPT-5.4 passes most of them, the 30B open model a third to a half, and the 7B open models almost none.

Post-training turns those checks into a teacher. The model writes several answers to the same prompt, each answer is scored (cheap geometry checks for static edits, a full simulation for every launch), and the model is pushed toward the answers that scored better than their siblings (GRPO), or toward the better answer of a pair (DPO, the cheaper baseline). The simulator is the final judge, on held-out tasks the model never trained on.

3. GRPO: learning from a group of its own answers

For each prompt the model writes a group of answers; each is scored, and the update favours the answers that beat their group's average. Below, a real group of sixteen answers from the untrained 7B model on a static edit, and thirteen launches the simulator has judged for one launch task.

4. DPO: learning from a better and a worse answer

The offline baseline: answers are sampled and scored once, then the model is trained to prefer the better answer of each pair. No simulator runs during training.

5. What is trained, and where the simulator sits

The 7.6 billion weights of the language model stay frozen. LoRA adds two small matrices beside every linear layer and trains only those, about one percent of the model, which is what lets a ten-thousand-token example fit one graphics card.

6. The first experiment

Static edits and launches of every difficulty from the benchmark, split into training tasks and held-out test tasks. Difficulty is measured by guessing: a task is easy when at least half of random answers pass, hard when fewer than 15 percent do. The untrained 7B model, GRPO and DPO are each judged by the simulator on the test tasks, next to the larger models below.

Post-training the 7B model: first results

Qwen2.5-7B was trained on 74 benchmark tasks (25 static edits and 49 launches of every difficulty) and then judged by the simulator on 48 held-out tasks it never trained on (different tasks of the same task types and rooms).
