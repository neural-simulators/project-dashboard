Post-training a 7B language model on simulator-verified scene edits

Standalone preview of the post-training blocks of the Neural Simulators page

Post-training an open model with the simulator as its teacher

Why post-train a language model?

Language models are the natural interface for instructions like "knock the red chair over" or "toss the ball into the box", and a model that also judges physics well could plan actions for a simulator or a robot. On the benchmark, GPT-5.4 meets the goal in about nine tasks out of ten. Open models, the ones anyone can run, inspect and fine-tune, do much worse: the 30B model passes a third to a half of the tasks and the 7B model almost none.

Post-training asks whether that gap can be closed with the simulator as the teacher instead of human labels. The model proposes several answers to a task, each answer is checked (geometric checks for a static edit, a full PhysX simulation for a launch), and small adapters are trained toward the answers that worked while the model's own weights stay frozen. The feedback is exact and unlimited; its cost is time, about 50 seconds per simulated launch, which a fast world model could cut to milliseconds. Every scene, answer and score below is a real record from the benchmark.

1. Abstract

The benchmark has two kinds of task. A static edit asks the model to move or turn named objects so that an instruction holds ("bring the red chair closer to the brown table"). A launch task asks for one initial velocity of one object so that something happens in the simulation ("toss the red ball over the barrier into the box"). Both are judged by deterministic checks and the PhysX simulator. GPT-5.4 passes most of them, the 30B open model a third to a half, and the 7B open models almost none.

Post-training turns those checks into a teacher. The model writes several answers to the same prompt, each answer is scored (cheap geometry checks for static edits, a full simulation for every launch), and the model is pushed toward the answers that scored better than their siblings (GRPO), or toward the better answer of a pair (DPO, the cheaper baseline). The simulator is the final judge, on held-out tasks the model never trained on.

2. Post-training in symbols

The notation of the World model tab carries over: \(G\) is the scene graph, \(a\) an action, \(\Phi\) the simulator and \(V\) a task's verdict. What is new is the language model as a policy.

\(x\)
the prompt: instruction, two pictures, the scene as data, the rules of the checks, the answer format
\(y = (y_1, \dots, y_L)\)
the answer, a sequence of tokens (JSON)
\(\pi_\theta(y \mid x) = \prod_{l} \pi_\theta(y_l \mid x, y_{\lt l})\)
the language model as a policy over answers
\(W = W_0 + BA\)
LoRA: every weight matrix \(W_0\) stays frozen and only the low-rank \(B A\) (rank 32) is trained, about 1 percent of the weights
\(\pi_{\text{ref}}\)
the model before training, the anchor for DPO
\(\psi(y)\)
the parser: the action \(a\) the answer states (new poses, or a launch velocity), or \(\bot\) when it breaks the answer contract
\(r(x, y)\)
the reward, always computed by code, never by a person or another model

The verified reward

For a launch the reward is built from the simulator's own verdict, with partial credit so that a group of failed answers can still be ranked (\(K\) checks, \(\mathbb{1}[\cdot]\) is 1 when true):

\[ r(x, y) = 0.1\,\mathbb{1}[\psi(y) \text{ parses}] + 0.2\,\mathbb{1}[\psi(y) \text{ is allowed}] + 0.4\, \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} g_k\big(\Phi(G, \psi(y))\big) + 0.3\, V\big(\Phi(G, \psi(y))\big) \]

For a static edit the simulator is replaced by geometric checks on the new poses (the requested relations, overlaps, support, tilt), which agree with PhysX's verdict on 60 of 61 stored answers: \(r = 0.05\, r_{\text{format}} + 0.10\, r_{\text{contract}} + 0.35\, r_{\text{relation}} + 0.20\, r_{\text{physical}} + 0.30\, r_{\text{pass}}\).

Three ways to train

SFT: imitate reference answers \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x, y^\star)}\, \log \pi_\theta(y^\star \mid x) \]

\(y^\star\) is the verified reference answer. The control arm: it teaches one way of answering and needs a reference for every task.

DPO: prefer the better of two own answers \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\theta) = -\,\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l)} \log \sigma\!\Big( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)} \Big) \]

For each prompt the start model writes several answers; the best and the worst become a pair \((y_w, y_l)\) when their rewards differ by at least 0.25. \(\beta = 0.1\). Offline: the simulator scores the answers once, before training.

GRPO: learn from a group of fresh answers \[ \hat A_g = \frac{r_g - \operatorname{mean}(r_{1:G})}{\operatorname{std}(r_{1:G}) + \delta}, \qquad J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}\Big[ \frac{1}{\sum_{g} |y_g|} \sum_{g=1}^{G} \sum_{l=1}^{|y_g|} \min\big(\rho_{g,l} \hat A_g,\ \operatorname{clip}(\rho_{g,l}, 1 \pm \epsilon)\, \hat A_g\big) \Big] - \beta\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_\theta \,\Vert\, \pi_{\text{ref}}\big) \]

For every prompt the current model writes \(G\) answers \(y_1, \dots, y_G\), each is scored, and each answer's advantage is its reward relative to its own group. \(\rho_{g,l}\) is the probability ratio of token \(l\) under the new and the sampling policy. In our run \(G = 8\), two prompts per update, 100 updates, \(\beta = 0\) and one gradient step per batch, so \(\rho = 1\), the clip never binds and the update is

\[ \nabla_\theta J = \frac{1}{\sum_{g} |y_g|} \sum_{g=1}^{G} \hat A_g \sum_{l=1}^{|y_g|} \nabla_\theta \log \pi_\theta\big(y_{g,l} \mid x, y_{g, \lt l}\big) \]

A group whose answers all score the same carries no signal (\(\hat A_g = 0\)); that was 32 percent of the groups on average. Online: every sampled launch is simulated during training.

6. What is trained, and where the simulator sits

The 7.6 billion weights of the language model stay frozen. LoRA adds two small matrices beside every linear layer and trains only those, about one percent of the model, which is what lets a ten-thousand-token example fit one graphics card.

8. Next: a world model as the judge

GRPO needs a verdict for every answer it samples, and today each launch verdict is a PhysX simulation. The proposal is to ask the world model instead, hosted on the same machine as the language model, so that a whole group of answers is judged in one batched pass:

\[ \hat r(x, y) = 0.1\,\mathbb{1}[\psi(y) \text{ parses}] + 0.2\,\mathbb{1}[\psi(y) \text{ is allowed}] + 0.4\, \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k} \hat g_k + 0.3\, \hat p(x, y) \] \[ \hat p(x, y) = \frac{1}{S} \sum_{s=1}^{S} V\big(Y^{(s)}\big), \qquad Y^{(s)} \sim p_\theta\big(\cdot \mid G, \psi(y)\big) \]

A generative world model gives \(S\) possible futures, so \(\hat p\) is a probability of success rather than a yes or no (\(S = 1\) for a deterministic one). DPO needs the same verdicts, only once and offline.

What accuracy is needed

Not a small distance error but the same verdicts as PhysX on the answers the policy actually writes. Two numbers decide: the agreement \(\alpha\) and the false-success rate \(\mathrm{FS}\), measured on the policy's own samples \(y \sim \pi_\theta\):

\[ \alpha = \Pr_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\big[\hat V = V\big], \qquad \mathrm{FS} = \Pr_{y \sim \pi_\theta}\big[\hat V = 1 \mid V = 0\big] \]

Random mistakes are tolerable. If every verdict were flipped independently with probability \(\eta \lt 1/2\), the expected reward would be \(\mathbb{E}[\hat r] = (1 - 2\eta)\, \mathbb{E}[r] + \eta\): answers keep their order and GRPO's signal only shrinks. Systematic false successes are not tolerable: training searches for answers the world model wrongly accepts and makes them more likely (model exploitation).

Safeguards

  • Audit. PhysX re-checks a share \(q\) of the answers in every update, in particular each group's best answer, the one GRPO reinforces most; audited answers keep PhysX's reward.
  • Doubt goes to PhysX. When the world model's samples disagree about the verdict (\(0 \lt \hat p \lt 1\)), that answer is simulated.
  • The judge keeps learning. Every audited answer becomes a new training rollout for the world model, on exactly the answers the policy now writes.
  • The final word stays with PhysX: trained models are judged by the simulator on held-out tasks, as now.

Where it stands. Not yet: in the end-to-end study the best world model recognizes 24 percent of real successes, and the toy models' predicted trajectories pass none of the benchmark's checks. The scaled study's best model (V5) gives the same verdict as PhysX on about 90 percent of its stored test simulations, but on the reviewer study's balanced items (two real successes and two failures per task) its predicted motion lets reviewers, and the rule checks, agree with PhysX only 61 to 68 percent of the time, and only 29 to 43 percent of real successes are found (Improved Results V5): a judge built on it today would under-reward rather than over-reward. Agreement and false successes on a policy's own answers are not measured yet. The switch is gated on \(\alpha\) and \(\mathrm{FS}\) measured on policy samples. Static edits already work this way: their reward replaces the simulator with geometric checks that agree with PhysX on 60 of 61 stored answers.

Two places to use it. The benchmark's launch tasks, as above, and the scaled study's action planner, for which GRPO with the learned simulator as the reward and PhysX checks on a quarter of the samples (\(q = 1/4\)) waits for a decision. Two world-model items decide how far such a judge can be trusted: actions written by language models in its training data (WM-H1; the policy's actions are what it must judge) and its speed at planning batch sizes (WM-H3).

9. Two more models, trained on Dataset V2: a planner and a reasoner

The sections above train one model to write a scene edit. Dataset V2 (46 mechanics families in scanned rooms, every action simulated together with its what-if branches) lets us train the two language-model parts of the loop the project is heading for. A planner chooses an action for a wanted outcome. A reasoner says what a changed world would do. The simulator sits between them, PhysX today and the world model once it judges like PhysX (section 8). A verifier written in code closes the loop: it checks the planner's outcome against the goal and the reasoner's answer against the changed run's record, and a model's opinion never enters it.

Where the training pairs come from

Dataset V2 records every action as a bundle: the factual run, and the same program run again in a changed world (a mass or friction changed, another material, another shape, an object removed, the actuator's command changed while it acts, an impulse or a thrust cut later). Each root has three such action programs on the same scene. The two models read the same tree in two directions.

The reasoner: counterfactual questions with measured answers

The prompt holds the scene (every movable object with its shape, size, mass, friction, restitution and pose, the joints and the room furniture nearby), the action program as applied, the observed run (its event facts, end state, first contacts and final positions), then the change in words together with every exact difference between the two manifests, and the questions. The questions are fixed by the factual record and the declared change alone: every measured quantity of the factual run is asked again for the changed run, and a comparison (more, less, about the same, not measurable) for every comparable kind. The answer is one JSON object; its target is read from the what-if record, so the label is exact and costs nothing beyond the dataset. A sentence of summary is asked for but carries no weight: the verifier scores only what it can measure.

The verified reward of an answer \[ r(x, y) = 0.05\, r_{\text{format}} + 0.35\, r_{\text{compare}} + 0.35\, r_{\text{quantity}} + 0.25\, r_{\text{outcome}} \]

Each part is in \([0, 1]\): the share of required fields present and well formed; the share of comparisons that match; the share of quantities within a tolerance (counts exactly, lengths within 5 cm or 10 percent, times within 0.15 s); and for the outcome the overlap of the moved and toppled sets, the agreement of the first contacts in pairs, order and time, and the share of final positions within 10 cm. An unfinished thinking block or no JSON scores zero. The same function is the SFT check, the GRPO reward and the evaluation metric.

Training \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}} = -\,\mathbb{E}\, \log \pi_\theta(y^\star \mid x), \qquad \text{then GRPO of section 2 with } r(x, y),\ G = 8,\ \beta = 0.02 \]

\(y^\star\) is the measured answer. GRPO then samples eight answers per prompt and moves toward the ones the verifier scores higher than their siblings; the reward is a lookup against measurements already on disk, so no simulator runs during training. Two baselines are scored on every evaluation row and are the numbers to beat: the no-change answer (copy the observed run, say "about the same" everywhere) and the family prior (the most common answer per family and question kind in the training split).

Why counterfactual pairs. A what-if asks for the mechanism, not for a memory of the observed run: the observed run is in the prompt, so copying it is the obvious shortcut, and the no-change baseline measures exactly how far that shortcut gets. The answer is discrete, the natural output of a language model, and it is the kind of question the world model's input contract cannot encode (a changed material, a removed object, a command that changes mid-action). On numeric what-ifs the world model's re-run will be the stronger instrument; the reasoner's value is language and the comparison, and that comparison is part of the evaluation.

The planner: an action for a wanted outcome

The prompt holds the initial scene graph (every body and constraint, with shared shapes and tables so the geometry stays compact), the rig's baseline program, the allowed parameters with their exact bounds, and the required outcome in words: "within the 6 s rollout, make the straight-line travel of the cart at least 0.76 m". The answer is one JSON object with four fields: which dimension to change (the drive, a release height or angle, a ramp offset, a vertical tug, a force field, or none), its value, and for pushes a lateral shift and a yaw. These are the dataset's own action vocabulary: parameters of a known mechanism, so every answer can be transcribed into the simulator and judged, and none can edit the scene itself.

The goal verdict \[ V_g(a) = \mathbb{1}\big[\, m_g\big(\Phi(G, a)\big) \in I_g \,\big] \]

\(m_g\) is one of eight measured quantities (final displacement, toppled count, containment, maximum height, maximum rotation, oscillation count, rebound height, time to rest) and \(I_g\) the required interval. The intervals are set per family from the quartiles of the training baselines' measurements, never from validation or test data. A missing or censored measurement gives no verdict rather than a failure.

Training: copy successes, then prefer them over failures \[ \mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}} = -\,\mathbb{E}_{V_g(a) = 1}\, \log \pi_\theta(a \mid G, g), \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} \text{ of section 2 on pairs } (a_w, a_l) \text{ with } V_g(a_w) = 1,\ V_g(a_l) = 0,\ \beta = 0.1 \]

Both actions of a pair act on the same scene toward the same goal, and both were simulated and physically valid: a valid rollout that misses the goal is the negative, an invalid or missing rollout is not. DPO starts from the SFT policy, which is also its reference. A trained planner is judged by committing one answer per held-out task and simulating it in PhysX, next to three controls: the unchanged program, random parameters inside the bounds, and the recorded actions rounded to three decimals.

Planned: the learned simulator as the environment, with retries \[ R = B\, \mathbb{1}[\text{goal met within } K \text{ attempts}] - c\, n_{\text{attempts}}, \qquad B \gt cK \]

Every attempt restarts from the same scene; the planner sees its earlier actions and their predicted measurements and proposes again. A GRPO-style update over whole episodes rewards reaching the goal in fewer queries. PhysX audits a sample of the policy's actions, as in section 8: a gap between the two simulators is a result, not a reason to swap the reward.

Why outcome-conditioned actions. A planner is the inverse of the simulator: given the outcome, find the action. Training it on recorded successes needs no new simulation, and three simulated programs per scene give successes and failures of the same scene and goal, which are exactly the pairs DPO needs. Keeping the answer to a few parameters of a known mechanism makes every answer verifiable and keeps the comparison with the benchmark's static edits and launches clean. The learned simulator enters only as the environment of the last stage, where its speed matters and PhysX keeps the final word.
