Post-training a 7B language model on simulator-verified scene edits

Standalone preview of the post-training blocks of the Neural Simulators page

Post-training an open model with the simulator as its teacher

Can a small open model learn 3D scene editing and launch planning from the simulator's verdicts instead of from human labels? The model's weights stay frozen; small adapters are trained with reinforcement learning, and the benchmark's own checks provide the reward. Every scene, answer and score below is a real record from the benchmark.

1. Abstract

The benchmark has two kinds of task. A static edit asks the model to move or turn named objects so that an instruction holds ("bring the red chair closer to the brown table"). A launch task asks for one initial velocity of one object so that something happens in the simulation ("toss the red ball over the barrier into the box"). Both are judged by deterministic checks and the PhysX simulator. GPT-5.4 passes most of them, the 30B open model about half, and the 7B open models almost none.

Post-training turns those checks into a teacher. The model writes several answers to the same prompt, each answer is scored (cheap geometry checks for static edits, a full simulation for every launch), and the model is pushed toward the answers that scored better than their siblings (GRPO), or toward the better answer of a pair (DPO, the cheaper baseline). The simulator is the final judge, on held-out tasks the model never trained on.

2. Training examples from the benchmark

Five real tasks, two static edits and three launches from easy to hard. Each one shows the task, then how the reference answer and the answers of three models fare in the simulator, then the reward each answer would receive in training. Pick an example or a step; the animation also plays through all of them.

3. GRPO: learning from a group of its own answers

For each prompt the model writes a group of answers; each is scored, and the update favours the answers that beat their group's average. Below, a real group of sixteen answers from the untrained 7B model on a static edit, and thirteen launches the simulator has judged for one launch task.

4. DPO: learning from a better and a worse answer

The offline baseline: answers are sampled and scored once, then the model is trained to prefer the better answer of each pair. No simulator runs during training.

5. What is trained, and where the simulator sits

The 7.6 billion weights of the language model stay frozen. LoRA adds two small matrices beside every linear layer and trains only those, about one percent of the model, which is what lets a ten-thousand-token example fit one graphics card.

6. The first experiment

Static edits and launches of every difficulty from the benchmark, split into training tasks and held-out test tasks. Difficulty is measured by guessing: a task is easy when at least half of random answers pass, hard when fewer than 15 percent do. The untrained 7B model, GRPO and DPO are each judged by the simulator on the test tasks, next to the larger models below.

Numbers for the trained models appear in the Results tab when the run finishes.

Post-training: first run and the run in progress

The first run trained the 7B model on ten static edits, one per task family, and judged every arm on those same ten tasks: an overfitting check of the machinery, not a benchmark result. It completed the untrained model's evaluation and the sampling that feeds the offline baselines before it was stopped to free GPUs. The run in progress is the real first experiment: a mix of static edits and launch tasks of every difficulty tier, split into training and held-out test tasks, with the simulator's verdicts for the untrained model, GRPO and DPO reported next to the campaign models on the same tasks.
